Meat Proxy

When forwarding AI output replaces thinking, the human becomes a relay. Context, verification, and accountability are what make the human contribution matter.

Gökhan Mergen My Blog: Post AGI
Oct 3, 2026 · 6 min read

The relay

You ask a colleague a question in Slack. A minute later the answer arrives, prefaced by two words: "Claude said:". Below it sits a wall of confident text that your colleague has, almost certainly, not read.

In August 2026 the German engineer Niklas Gruhn gave this person a name. In a short post, Don't be a meat proxy, he pointed out that he could ask Claude himself, faster and with better control of the context, and then wrote the line that went viral: "I don't need a meat proxy in between." Within weeks the phrase had a Hacker News thread, coverage in Futurism and Cybernews, and a dictionary-style definition on kottke.org: a person who forwards AI output without reading, understanding, or validating it.

The insult works because of the word "meat." A proxy is a technical role; meat is what is left of a person once you subtract the thinking. The meat proxy is not accused of using AI. Everyone uses AI. The accusation is that, between the model and the recipient, nothing happened inside the human at all.

What a proxy is supposed to add

In networking, a proxy is a respectable thing. It sits between a client and a server and does work: it caches, filters, authenticates, translates, logs. A proxy that passes packets through untouched is called transparent, and nobody deploys one for its own sake.

Information theory makes the point sharper. The data processing inequality says no step in a chain can increase what the output tells you about the source; a relay can only preserve information or lose it. A pure forwarder is, at best, a lossless wire. Its contribution to the conversation is zero bits, plus latency.

So where can a human in the loop add anything? Only through side information the model lacked. Three kinds matter:

  • Context: knowing which question was really being asked, what the team already tried, and which answer would be wrong for reasons that never appear in the prompt.
  • Verification: checking the claim against the code, the data, or the world. Gruhn's own example was a sentence of jargon so dense he had to look up nearly every word, the kind of output that sounds right precisely because nobody can quickly falsify it.
  • Accountability: being the person who can be asked "why?" tomorrow and answer in their own words.

The meat proxy supplies none of the three. Worse, the cost does not vanish; it moves. Generating text is now nearly free, but reading and checking it is not, so the proxy quietly exports the expensive half of the job to the next person in the chain. As one write-up of Simon Willison's warning framed it, the proxy does not remove work from a conversation; it hands the hard part to someone else.

Gruhn's code-review case is the purest version. Paste the ticket into an agent, paste the reviewers' comments back in, iterate until approval. Code ships. But who implemented it? The reviewers did, using the agent, with the author serving as a conduit.

The economics of the signature

Why would a competent person do this? Partly fatigue, but mostly incentives. When an organization rewards AI adoption by counting outputs, forwarding becomes the locally rational move. The meat proxy is a close cousin of "workslop", the polished artifact that collapses on inspection: one person generates it, another forwards it, and a third does the checking that should have happened two steps earlier.

There is a deeper story, and it is about signals. For most of history, explaining something in your own words was costly, and the cost was the point. Like a diploma in Spence's job-market model, prose was evidence: you could not describe a race condition clearly without having understood it. Gruhn says almost exactly this. Rewriting the answer yourself, he argues, is a decent certificate that you read, understood, and validated it.

Generative models drove the cost of fluent prose toward zero, and the signal went with it. Fluency no longer certifies understanding. What still certifies it is whatever remains costly: being specific about this codebase, being right in a way that can be checked, and being willing to sign. The signature is the last scarce good. A meat proxy spends it without having earned it, which is why the label stings. It describes someone whose name is on the message and whose mind is not.

The proxy's defenders make the critics' case for them. One engineer, quoted in a roundup of the debate, argued that asking a meat proxy is no different from asking Claude. Precisely. A person who is perfectly substitutable by a chat window has just told the labor market their marginal product. No wonder some on X called it the biggest career risk of 2026.

Where the line actually is

It would be easy to turn all this into a purity test: real professionals write every word themselves. That is wrong, and it is not what Gruhn said. He told people to prompt freely. The line is not whether a model wrote the first draft. It is whether a human stands behind the last one.

Seen this way, the meat proxy is not new. The manager who forwards a consultant's deck unread, the student who submits a borrowed essay, the analyst who pastes a sell-side note into an investment memo: all were proxies long before the models arrived. What changed is friction. Copying used to require finding a source, and delegating required a person on the other end. Now the source is infinite, instant, and fluent in every domain, so the temptation is constant and the tell is faint.

Cory Doctorow's image of the reverse centaur, a machine head on a human body, names the darker version. The centaur uses the machine to extend human judgment. The reverse centaur lends the machine a body: hands to paste, a name to sign, a seat in the meeting. As agents improve, institutions will feel this pull, because a model cannot be fired, sued, or licensed, and a person can. The danger is not that people will use AI. It is that people will be hired to be the accountable surface of decisions they did not make.

The practical test is short. Before you send, ask whether you could defend the thing line by line if the model vanished. Could you explain line 40? If not, you are not the author. You are the cable.

Coda: Escape Velocity

Then there is the video.

On September 25, a creator who posts as anabology shared a five-minute music video with a one-line caption. He had given Claude Opus 5.5 "donald's prompt, Midjourney, and a moodboard," gone to sleep, and woken twelve hours later to the result. The piece, later uploaded as SLOPCORE: ESCAPE VELOCITY, is a cyberpunk music video staged partly as a runway show about AI: figures, lyrics, collage, grain and transitions hitting on the beat. It passed roughly 19 million views, and Elon Musk reposted it, reportedly saying that this time he truly felt the AGI (coverage).

I had talked about this in an earlier blog post. This is more or less what I call the AI awakening happening in real time.
