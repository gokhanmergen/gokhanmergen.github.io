When Gallup released its annual governance survey in October 2026, one number stopped observers cold. Only 51% of Americans now express a "great deal" or a "fair amount" of trust in the American people as a whole to make judgments about the issues facing the country. In 1976, just two years after Richard Nixon resigned in disgrace, that figure stood at 86%. Across half a century of [Gallup polling](https://news.gallup.com/poll/715361/trust-federal-government-sinks-new-depths.aspx), no reading has ever been lower; the full series is in the survey's [topline](https://news.gallup.com/file/poll/715379/261013Trust.pdf). Cynicism toward politicians (trusted by just 42%) and the press (33%) is a familiar American habit. Losing faith in one another is something altogether new—and far more dangerous.

The milestone is ominous because, as [Axios put it](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/14/american-trust-voters-government-gallup), "Americans are losing faith in their fellow voters," not only in their leaders. Confronted with this erosion, commentators usually reach for an intuitive culprit. As economist Noah Smith [put it](https://x.com/Noahpinion/status/2115153435737862252): "Social media really messes with liberal democracy." It is a seductive, almost comforting diagnosis. If smartphones and algorithmic feeds have scrambled our civic judgment, then the underlying architecture of the republic remains sound; repair the digital wiring, and civic peace will return.

The empirical record tells a far more unsettling story, beginning with a cross-national paradox. The United States is not the most polarized democracy in the world, but it has polarized faster than almost any of its peers. When economists Levi Boxell, Matthew Gentzkow, and Jesse Shapiro [tracked partisan hostility](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01160) across twelve advanced democracies over four decades, they found that animus surged more dramatically in the United States than anywhere else—while actually declining in six of the twelve countries. Global forces that struck every rich democracy at the same time—the birth of the commercial internet, the ubiquity of smartphones, manufacturing import shocks from China, and shifting migration flows—cannot by themselves explain why America uniquely pulled apart while comparable democracies held together.

To weigh the competing explanations, this essay puts each of them to three empirical tests:

1. **Timing:** A genuine cause must precede the consequence it purports to explain.
2. **Causal Identification:** Any serious account must separate the true effect of an alleged driver from the self-selection of the people drawn to it, relying on randomized experiments and natural shocks rather than raw correlations.
3. **Cross-National Fit:** A shock common to every rich democracy cannot, on its own, explain why only some of them polarized—though it could still matter where parties and institutions differ.

The record does not show sudden algorithmic poisoning. It shows a half-century realignment of American social and political identities: **The Great Rotation**. How that realignment turned into hostility is less certain. This essay proposes one account, in which identities and institutions reinforced each other. A [companion paper](/pubs/the-great-rotation/alternative-hypotheses.pdf) describes two others that fit the same evidence about as well, one led by party elites and activists and one accelerated by media, and the data assembled here cannot choose among them.

For most of the twentieth century, the American party system divided voters primarily over economics. Crucially, race, religious observance, educational attainment, and geography cut across the partisan divide. Northern and Southern Democrats held irreconcilable views on racial hierarchy; Wall Street moderates and Main Street conservatives contested the soul of the Republican Party. Over the past fifty years, however, four societal cleavages—race, religion, education, and place—slid one after another into alignment behind the two major parties, while the old divide of economic class faded. A party label ceased to denote a mere preference on marginal tax rates or regulatory scope; it came to signify a comprehensive cultural, moral, and geographic identity.

In the account proposed here, once those identities stacked atop one another, political rivals stopped looking like fellow citizens with mistaken economic theories and began looking like existential threats to one's way of life. America's institutional container—a rigid, winner-take-all two-party duopoly operating under razor-thin congressional majorities—did not absorb this shock. Instead, it compressed every social and cultural dispute onto a single binary battlefield. Today, an electoral machinery dominated by uncompetitive safe seats and low-turnout primaries rewards political entrepreneurs who stoke existential dread. In that self-sustaining loop, the public's collapsing faith in fellow voters becomes understandable: it may be, in part, a verdict on the candidates our neighbors keep electing, even if it rests on an incomplete picture of what those neighbors actually believe.

## 1. What has changed, and what has not

Much of the confusion surrounding American polarization persists because a single word is routinely used to describe four distinct phenomena: policy disagreement among ordinary citizens, the sorting of social identities and ideologies into party camps, mutual emotional hostility between partisans, and legislative divergence among elected officials. Separating these four threads reveals a striking pattern: party and ideology came into close alignment while moderate self-identification remained common. Self-labels cannot rule out changes in policy views, but the clearest change is that Americans sorted their identities into rival camps.

Vigorous disagreement over tax rates, healthcare, and regulation is not the enemy of self-government; negotiating competing interests is the very purpose of democratic politics. The pathology begins when partisanship fuses with social identity, turning rivals into moral enemies. The clearest barometer of that psychological shift is the American National Election Studies (ANES) "feeling thermometer," which since 1978 has asked partisans to rate both parties on a scale from 0 (coldest) to 100 (warmest). The decisive clue lies in which line moved.

![Partisans grew colder toward the other party, not warmer to their own. Line chart, 1978–2020: partisans' average rating of their own party stays near 75 on a 0–100 scale, while their rating of the other party falls from 47 to 19.](polarization-feeling-thermometers.png)

*Fig. 1 — Almost all of the widening comes from cooling toward the other side. Source: ANES 1978–2020, from the [replication files of Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2024)](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/E8Q4BX). The 2024 study shows no retreat: 21 for the other party and 70 for one's own, among partisans including leaners ([University of Arizona Voter Project](https://voterproject.arizona.edu/anes)).*

Over more than four decades, Americans have not grown any fonder of their own party; in-party warmth has hovered near 75 since the late 1970s (Figure 1). Instead, feelings toward the opposing party plunged from a lukewarm 47 in 1978 to an icy 19 in 2020, which accounts for 98% of the widening gap (28.4 of its 28.9 points). This is what political scientists call *negative partisanship*: today's coalitions are bound together less by affection for their own leaders or platform than by shared loathing of the alternative. "Republicans and Democrats increasingly dislike, even loathe, their opponents," as Shanto Iyengar and colleagues [put it in 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfs038).

That antipathy has spilled out of the voting booth and into intimate social life.

![From disagreement to contempt. Three panels: the share of partisans with a very unfavorable view of the other party rises from 21% of Republicans and 17% of Democrats in 1994 to 62% and 54% in 2022; the share calling the other side immoral, closed-minded and dishonest rises between 2016 and 2022; and the share unhappy about a child marrying across party lines rises from about 5% in 1960 to between a third and a half in 2010.](polarization-from-disagreement-to-contempt.png)

*Fig. 2 — In 1960, 5% of Republicans and 4% of Democrats said they would be displeased if their child married across party lines; by 2010, 49% and 33% said so (wordings differ across these surveys). Sources: [Pew Research Center, 2022](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2022/08/09/as-partisan-hostility-grows-signs-of-frustration-with-the-two-party-system/); [Iyengar, Sood & Lelkes (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfs038); [YouGov, 2020](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/32171-america-speaks-what-do-they-think-about-cross-part).*

In 1960, fewer than one partisan in twenty cared whether their child married across party lines; by 2010, a third of Democrats and half of Republicans said they would be unhappy about it, and in 2020, 38% in each party said they would be upset (Figure 2; the wordings differ). Moral condemnation has escalated just as sharply. Between 2016 and 2022 alone, [Pew Research Center surveys](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2022/08/09/as-partisan-hostility-grows-signs-of-frustration-with-the-two-party-system/) found that the share of Republicans calling Democrats more immoral than other Americans surged from 47% to 72%, while the share of Democrats saying the same of Republicans jumped from 35% to 63%.

Did this emotional chasm open because ordinary voters adopted extreme policy views? Self-described ideology suggests that is at most part of the story. The congressional record, which reaches back much further than the surveys, shows the parties in Washington pulling apart from the late 1970s.

![Congress began pulling apart in the late 1970s. Line charts, 1879–2025: the distance between the parties' average DW-NOMINATE scores in the House and Senate is lowest in the 1940s and rises steadily from the late 1970s to 0.91 in the House in 2025, while the share of House moderates falls from 40% in 1971 to 9% in 2025.](polarization-congress.png)

*Fig. 3 — More than half (55%) of the House's widening since 1977 came before Fox News launched in 1996, and moderates fell from 40% of the House in 1971 to 9% in 2025. Source: [Voteview](https://voteview.com/data) member scores; computed for this essay.*

Measured by DW-NOMINATE scores—which place every congressional roll-call vote on a liberal-to-conservative scale—legislative polarization reached its twentieth-century low in the 1940s and stayed muted through the 1960s (Figure 3). The modern divergence began in the late 1970s. Centrist lawmakers (those scoring between −0.25 and +0.25) accounted for 40% of the House in 1971; by 2025, they had dwindled to 9%. The ideological distributions of the two parties last overlapped in the House in 2001–02 and in the Senate in 2003–04.

Among ordinary voters, however, self-described ideological extremism grew only modestly.

![Moderates stayed common. Party and ideology aligned. Line charts from the General Social Survey, 1974–2024: the share calling themselves moderate slips from 40% to 37% and the share calling themselves extremely liberal or conservative rises from 4% to 10%, while the correlation between party identification and ideology rises from 0.17 to 0.63.](polarization-sorted-not-extreme.png)

*Fig. 4 — Moderates were 40% of Americans in 1974 and 37% in 2024, while the correlation between party and ideology rose from 0.17 to 0.63: moderate self-identification stayed common as party and ideology aligned. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay.*

Across fifty years of the General Social Survey, the share of Americans calling themselves political moderates held remarkably steady, slipping only from 40% in 1974 to 37% in 2024; the share calling themselves extremely liberal or extremely conservative rose from 4% to 10% (Figure 4). Self-placement is an imperfect gauge of policy views: the meanings of "moderate," "liberal" and "conservative" shift over time, and the extremes did grow. Meanwhile the correlation between party identification and self-reported ideology more than tripled, rising from 0.17 to 0.63, and liberal Republicans and conservative Democrats became rare. Partisan alignment increased sharply while moderate self-identification remained common; the issue questions in Section 3 show that the parties' gaps on specific policies widened as well (see Appendix A for formal definitions and the Fiorina–Abramowitz debate).

As this sorting hardened, public trust collapsed in two distinct waves separated by three decades.

![Americans lost faith in their institutions first, then in each other. Line charts: trust in the federal government falls from 77% in 1964 to about a quarter by 1980 and 17% in 2025; trust in the American people's political judgment falls from 86% in 1976 to 51% in 2026; and the share saying most people can be trusted falls from 47% in 1972 to 25% in 2024.](polarization-trust.png)

*Fig. 5 — Trust in the federal government fell from 77% in 1964 to about a quarter by 1980, through Vietnam, Watergate and stagflation, and is 17% today. The share saying most people can be trusted fell from 47% in 1972 to 25% in 2024. Sources: [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/); Gallup Governance topline, September 2026; General Social Survey.*

The first collapse was *vertical*: battered by Vietnam, Watergate, and 1970s stagflation, public trust in the federal government plunged from 77% in 1964 to roughly a quarter by 1980, before drifting down to 17% today (Figure 5). That institutional crash came before the thermometer gap began its steep climb in the 1990s, so rising partisan hostility is unlikely to explain it. The second collapse was *horizontal*, and it shows up in two related but distinct measures. Interpersonal trust—the General Social Survey's question whether "most people can be trusted"—eroded from 47% in 1972 to about a quarter in 2024 (the exact recent level depends on how the question was asked; see the [Methods and Data appendix](/pubs/the-great-rotation/methods-and-data.pdf)). Confidence in the American people's *political judgment*, which Gallup measures, stayed at 70% or higher through 2009 and fell steeply after that. The first asks whether strangers will treat you fairly; the second asks whether your fellow citizens choose wisely at the ballot box. Section 8 returns to the second.

## 2. The usual suspects: cable news and social media

Popular commentary blames this civic unraveling almost reflexively on media technology: 24-hour partisan cable news, algorithmic social feeds, and smartphone notifications. Digital media may intensify political hostility, distort perceptions, and reward factional entrepreneurs. But on all three tests—timing, causal identification, and cross-national fit—they arrived too late, and moved hostility too little in experiments, to explain the origins of the central trends examined here.

The most immediate problem is chronology. A cause cannot arrive decades after its supposed effect is already well underway.

![The timing test: most of the change came before the feed. Six small line charts of how much of each trend's total change had happened by each year, with the years since 2011 shaded: by 2011, distrust of government was 96% complete, House polarization 84%, interpersonal distrust 71%, sorting 67%, coldness toward the other party 72% (the in-party minus out-party gap 73%), and distrust of the people's judgment 55%. Dots show single readings. A bottom panel shows internet, social media and smartphone adoption rising mostly after 2000.](polarization-timing-test.png)

*Fig. 6 — How much of each trend's total change had happened by each year; shading marks the years since a majority of adults used social media; dots are single readings. The feelings panel shows both coldness toward the other party and the in-party minus out-party gap. A descriptive chronology, not a measure of causes. Sources: Pew, Voteview, GSS, ANES and Gallup; computed for this essay.*

Tracking each trend from its modern baseline to its latest reading shows that several were well under way before contemporary social media existed (Figure 6; see Table B.1 in Appendix B for exact milestones):

- **Distrust of the federal government** had already completed half of its modern rise by 1973, 84% by the time Fox News went on the air in October 1996, and 96% by 2011, the year a majority of American adults first used social media.
- **Polarization in Congress** crossed its halfway mark by 1994—two years before Fox News or MSNBC launched—reached 59% by 1996, and was 84% complete by 2011, when smartphone ownership stood near 35%.
- **Generalized social distrust** was half complete by 1992 and 71% complete by 2011.
- **Party–ideology sorting** crossed its halfway mark by 2004—the year Facebook was founded in a college dorm room—and was 67% complete by 2011.
- **Coldness toward the other party** was half complete by 2006 and 72% complete by 2011; the in-party minus out-party gap, which also counts feelings toward one's own party, was 73% complete. Its change between 2006 and 2011 was faster than in the decade before, so the feed era is not irrelevant to the later part of the rise.

These shares describe a chronology, not causes. Each is a share of the net change between two smoothed endpoints; the year half of adults used social media is not the year exposure began; and centered smoothing uses readings from after each date. What the timing establishes is that several of these trends began well before contemporary social media; it cannot say how much of the later rise social media caused.

Partisan cable news did leave a measurable footprint. Exploiting the staggered rollout of Fox News across local cable systems, [Stefano DellaVigna and Ethan Kaplan](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.122.3.1187) found that it shifted towns 0.4 to 0.7 percentage points toward Republicans in 2000, and [Elliott Ash and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2024.105256) find that it has raised Republican vote shares by at least half a point in recent elections. Yet by [the most careful structural estimates](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160812), cable news can plausibly account for the public's modest rise in ideological polarization, not the far faster polarization of Congress; its clearest documented effect on politicians is that [House members whose districts got Fox became less supportive of President Clinton](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022381614000425). And it arrived two decades after the congressional parties began pulling apart.

The demographic pattern points the same way. If algorithmic feeds and online echo chambers were the primary engine of polarization, the heaviest users—young, digitally native adults—should have polarized fastest.

![The least-online Americans sorted the most. Left: the correlation of party with ideology by age group, 1974–2024, rising fastest among those 65 and older. Right: in 2024, 92% of 18- to 29-year-olds used social media the day before against 69% of those 65 and older, yet 79% of the oldest partisans were ideologically consistent against 67% of the youngest.](polarization-least-online-sorted-most.png)

*Fig. 7 — In 2024, 92% of 18- to 29-year-olds had used social media the day before, against 69% of those 65 and older, yet the oldest were the most ideologically consistent. Sources: GSS; [Cooperative Election Study 2024](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/X11EP6); computed for this essay.*

The data do not fit that prediction (Figure 7). Between 1996 and 2016, the correlation between party and ideology in the GSS rose more among Americans aged 65 and older (+0.21) than among those aged 18 to 39 (+0.14), though the difference between the two increases is within sampling error. On an index of eight polarization measures over the same period, [Boxell, Gentzkow, and Shapiro](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706588114) found that polarization grew about twice as fast among seniors (+0.47 index points) as among young adults (+0.23)—precisely the cohort least plugged into digital media. In the 2024 Cooperative Election Study, 92% of 18-to-29-year-olds reported using social media the previous day compared with 69% of seniors, yet 79% of partisans aged 65 and older held consistently left-of-center or right-of-center views across both economics and culture, compared with 67% of young adults. Age comparisons mix the effects of age, generation and the times people grew up in, so they cannot by themselves rule out a media effect; they do show that the least-online group is not the least sorted.

Cross-national comparisons point the same way. Every wealthy democracy adopted broadband internet, smartphones, and social networks during the same historical window, yet their civic trajectories diverged sharply.

![Every rich democracy got the internet. Only some polarized. Bar chart of the trend in affective polarization in twelve democracies, from +5.6 thermometer points a decade in the United States to −3.7 in Germany, falling in six countries; scatter plots show the trend against internet growth (r = −0.48) and against elite polarization (r = +0.73, ten countries).](polarization-cross-country.png)

*Fig. 8 — Hostility rose fastest in the United States, which had the slowest growth in internet use of the twelve, and fell in six countries. Source: replication data of [Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01160); correlations computed for this essay.*

Among twelve OECD democracies tracked from roughly 1980 to 2020, the United States experienced the steepest increase in partisan hostility (+5.6 thermometer points per decade) despite recording the slowest growth in internet use over the period; across the twelve, faster internet growth went with *less* rising hostility (r = −0.48, though with an interval that includes zero; Figure 8). In half of the countries—Japan, Australia, Britain, Norway, Sweden, and Germany—affective polarization actually fell as citizens went online. The strongest association was not with internet adoption but with whether a country's party elites pulled apart (r = +0.73 across the ten countries with data), a relationship Section 4 examines with due caution.

Randomized experiments give a more nuanced picture. The studies considered here are those that randomly changed what US users saw on social media, or took them off it, and reported effects on affective polarization, published or posted by October 2026. Before the 2018 midterms, economists paid Facebook users to deactivate their accounts for four weeks. During the 2020 election, academic teams partnered with Meta to run large randomized trials across tens of thousands of Facebook and Instagram users—switching feeds to reverse-chronological order, stripping out reshared posts or down-ranking like-minded partisan sources for about three months, or paying users to deactivate their accounts for six weeks. Two newer studies changed feeds without the platforms' cooperation.

![Changing the feed moves partisan dislike, but only a little. Top: dot chart of eight randomized experiments on social media; every estimated effect on affective polarization lies within about 0.06 standard deviations of zero, one is significant before but not after the study's adjustment for multiple outcomes, and a 2024 preprint reports −0.03 standard deviations. Bottom: in thermometer points, changing exposure to hostile posts on X moved feelings toward the other party by more than 2 points and alternative feed rankers by 1.5 points, against a 28.9-point rise in the ANES gap from 1978 to 2020.](polarization-experiments.png)

*Fig. 9 — Most estimates are within a few hundredths of a standard deviation of zero. Newer experiments find small but detectable effects—about 2 thermometer points from changing exposure to hostile posts—against a 29-point rise since 1978. Sources: [Allcott et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658), [Allcott et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2321584121), [Guess et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abp9364), [Nyhan et al. (2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06297-w), [Piccardi et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adu5584), [Stray et al. (2026, preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19626).*

The 2018–2020 experiments found small effects on affective polarization: every point estimate lies within about 0.06 standard deviations of zero (Figure 9). One—quitting Facebook for six weeks in 2020—reduced it by 0.03 standard deviations, significant before but not after the study's adjustment for testing many outcomes. Deactivating Facebook or changing its feed altered news knowledge and time on the platform far more than it altered partisan feeling. The newer studies show that feed design *can* move hostility. In a ten-day experiment on X during the 2024 campaign, [reranking feeds to show more or fewer posts expressing partisan animosity](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adu5584) shifted feelings toward the other party by more than 2 points on the 100-point thermometer. And in a [six-month experiment across Facebook, Reddit and X](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19626), still a preprint, alternative ranking algorithms reduced affective polarization by 0.03 standard deviations, including a 1.5-point narrowing of the thermometer gap. Those effects are real but small next to the 29-point rise in that gap since 1978; and experiments lasting days to months cannot measure what years of exposure might do.

None of this absolves social media platforms of civic harm. Out-group animosity is uniquely lucrative online: in [a study of millions of Facebook and Twitter posts](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118), each word referencing the political out-group raised the odds that a post would be shared by roughly 67%, and in [a 2026 study of X](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10098-2), switching on the platform's ranking algorithm shifted users' political attitudes within weeks, even though it left partisan dislike unchanged. Digital platforms warp perceptions of public opinion and help insurgent politicians raise money and attention outside party gatekeepers. The evidence supports a narrower conclusion than either "social media did it" or "social media is harmless": social media is unlikely to be the original or a sufficient explanation of America's long-run polarization, but what feeds show can move partisan hostility, and the platforms may well amplify it. To find the forces that began it, we must look beneath the screen at the demographic foundations of the electorate.

## 3. The Great Rotation: which divides moved, and which faded

In the mid-twentieth century, knowing whether an American voted Democratic or Republican revealed remarkably little about how they worshiped, how far they had gone in school, where they lived, or where they stood on racial equality. Both parties were sprawling, internally contradictory coalitions. The New Deal Democratic majority held together Southern segregationists and Northern urban labor liberals; the Republican Party bridged Main Street Midwestern conservatives and Wall Street Northeastern moderates. Because race, religion, education, and geography cut *across* party lines, social life moderated political conflict. Your opponent on tax rates might sit beside you in church on Sunday or send their children to the same rural school.

Over the past half-century, that changed in two ways at once. Cultural divides lined up behind the parties: race first, then religion, then education and place. Meanwhile economic class, the divide that had organized the New Deal party system, stopped predicting party. The line between the parties did not simply harden where it was. It turned, from class toward culture. Alan Abramowitz calls the end state [*The Great Alignment*](https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctvhrczh3); this essay calls the process the Great Rotation.

### Testing the divides instead of choosing them

Any short list of divides invites the question: why these? To avoid choosing the ones that fit the story, this section starts from a list fixed in advance by the classic theory of social cleavages. Seymour Martin Lipset and Stein Rokkan [traced Western party systems](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Party+Systems+and+Voter+Alignments+Lipset+Rokkan) to four old conflicts: owners against workers, church against state, countryside against city, and the national center against its periphery (in America, the South). To those four the list adds race, education, gender, age and family status, and, where the General Social Survey asked about them, gun ownership, union membership, birthplace and military service. That makes 23 characteristics. Nine of them are fixed by birth or upbringing, such as race or the religion someone was raised in, so adult partisanship cannot have shaped them.

Figure 10 shows how much each characteristic split the parties in the 1970s and how much it splits them now.

![The divides that moved, and the one that faded. Arrow chart of 23 characteristics, showing how much more Republican partisans with each characteristic were than those without it in the 1970s and in 2021–24. Class characteristics move toward zero: calling oneself middle or upper class from +15 to 0 points, top income third from +10 to +3, self-employed from +10 to +6, union household from −14 to −9. Cultural characteristics move toward the Republicans: gun in the household from +7 to +29, fundamentalist denomination from −7 to +19, weekly attendance from +3 to +19, no four-year degree from −11 to +14, father without a degree from −18 to +12, small town or countryside from +8 to +21, married from +6 to +14, children from +3 to +16, born in the United States from +1 to +18, veteran from 0 to +14. Having no religion moves from −7 to −26 and being a woman from −1 to −10. White moves from +25 to +34.](polarization-divides-all.png)

*Fig. 10 — Each arrow runs from the 1970s to 2021–24. Characteristics in italics are fixed by birth or upbringing. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay.*

Three patterns stand out.

- **Cultural divides opened.** In the 1970s, partisans with a gun at home were 7 points more Republican than those without one; now they are 29 points more Republican. Members of fundamentalist Protestant denominations went from 7 points *less* Republican than other partisans to 19 points more. The same shift appears for weekly churchgoers (3 points to 19), people without a four-year degree (−11 to +14), residents of small towns and the countryside (8 to 21), married people (6 to 14), parents (3 to 16), the native-born (1 to 18) and veterans (0 to 14). On the other side, Americans with no religion went from 7 to 26 points less Republican, and women from 1 to 10 points.
- **The class divide faded.** In the 1970s, partisans who called themselves middle or upper class were 15 points more Republican than those who called themselves working or lower class. By 2021–24 there was no difference at all. The top third of earners went from 10 points more Republican to 3, the self-employed from 10 to 6, and union households from 14 points less Republican to 9. Most of this fading came after 2014, and it shows up equally among people interviewed in person and online.
- **Race stayed the widest divide.** It was already the largest in the 1970s (25 points) and widened modestly (34 points).

Many of these characteristics travel together. Evangelicals are disproportionately white, Southern and rural, for example, so their gaps overlap. To see which divides carry weight of their own, the analysis puts all of them into one model and asks a simple question: how much does knowing each set of characteristics improve a prediction of whether a partisan is a Republican? The model is judged on respondents it has not seen, and credit is shared out fairly among the sets (by Shapley values: each set's average gain when added to every possible combination of the others). The measure does not care about direction. A trait that predicts Democratic identification earns as much credit as one that predicts Republican identification.

![What predicts a partisan's party: class gave way to race and religion. Nine small line charts, 1970s to 2021–24, of each set of characteristics' contribution to predicting party. Race rises from 2.0 to 6.3 and religion from 0.2 to 5.9; education rises from 0.3 to 1.4, place from 0.4 to 1.0, family status from 0.0 to 1.2 and gender from 0.0 to 1.0; class falls from 1.8 to 0.1.](polarization-predictors.png)

*Fig. 11 — All nine sets together improve the prediction of party by 5% in the 1970s and 18% in 2021–24; each panel shows one set's share. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay; method in the [Methods and Data appendix](/pubs/the-great-rotation/methods-and-data.pdf).*

Figure 11 gives the answer.

- **Social position predicts party far better than it used to.** All nine sets together improve the prediction by 5% in the 1970s and by 18% in 2021–24. That is still modest. Demography is not destiny, and most of the uncertainty about any one partisan remains.
- **In the 1970s, race and class did nearly all the work, in roughly equal measure** (38% and 34% of the total). **In 2021–24, race (35%) and religion (33%) dominate.** Education (8%), family status (7%), place (6%) and gender (6%) follow. Class contributes almost nothing (under 1%), and its decline is clear of sampling error.
- **Race counts for more partly because more people sit on each side of it.** The race gap widened only modestly, but the nonwhite share of partisans doubled, from 12% to 24%, and a divide predicts more when it splits the population more evenly. Religion's contribution rose for two reasons: the gaps widened, and Americans with no religion grew from 6% to 25% of partisans.
- **Guns are a strong fifth marker.** The question was asked of only about two-thirds of respondents, so it is tested separately. Added to the model, gun ownership ranks third of ten sets in 2021–24, with 19% of the total, up from sixth (3%) in the 1970s. Union membership moves the other way, from third (18%) to seventh (3%).

### Why these four divides

The scan largely vindicates the four divides this essay follows: race, religion, education and place. Race, religion and education are the three strongest sets today. Family status, place and gender come next, nearly tied, and place's gain since the 1970s is too small to separate from sampling error once the other divides are held fixed. Together the four reproduce 86% of the predictive power of all nine sets in 2021–24, against 65% in the 1970s, when class still mattered.

The scan also amends the story in two ways. First, religion is better measured by denomination and secularism as well as by attendance. The simple four-trait count used later in this section, which measures religion by attendance alone, captures about three-fifths (62%) of the full model's power. Second, the rotation has a second half that the four divides leave out: economic class stopped predicting party. As recently as the 2000s, richer voters were reliably more Republican, especially in poorer states, as Andrew Gelman showed in [*Red State, Blue State, Rich State, Poor State*](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400832118). In the General Social Survey, the top third of earners was 11 to 17 points more Republican in every survey from 2004 to 2014. That gap has all but vanished. Half of this pattern is familiar: the more educated moving left is what [Amory Gethin, Clara Martínez-Toledano, and Thomas Piketty](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab036) find across Western democracies and what [Herbert Kitschelt and Philipp Rehm](https://doi.org/10.1177/0032329219861215) trace among white Americans. The other half goes further than Gethin and his colleagues found through 2020, when high earners in most Western democracies still leaned right; in the General Social Survey, by 2021–24, they barely do. The late timing fits the account of John Sides, Michael Tesler, and Lynn Vavreck in [*Identity Crisis*](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400888306), in which the 2016 campaign made attitudes about race, immigration and national identity more central to the vote.

### How the four lined up, one after another

![The four divides that now line up behind party. Four small line charts, 1972–2024, of the Republican share of partisans in paired groups: white versus nonwhite (gap of 25 points in the 1970s, 34 in 2021–24), weekly churchgoers versus those who rarely or never attend (5 to 25), no college degree versus college graduates (−11 to +14), and small towns and countryside versus cities (15 to 29).](polarization-four-divides.png)

*Fig. 12 — Each panel compares the Republican share of two groups' partisans. The race gap opened by the 1980s; religion widened through the 1990s and 2000s; education reversed and the urban–rural gap doubled, mostly after 2012. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay.*

Half a century of General Social Survey data reveals how this realignment unfolded in four sequential acts (Figure 12):

- **Act I: Race (1964–1980s).** A Democratic president's push for the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 shattered the New Deal coalition, the story Edward Carmines and James Stimson tell in [*Issue Evolution*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691023311/issue-evolution). Racially conservative white Southerners who had anchored the Democratic Party since Reconstruction migrated steadily toward the GOP—a defection that, according to [Ilyana Kuziemko and Ebonya Washington](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20161413), accounts for the entire drop in Southern white Democratic identification between 1958 and 1980. The racial gap between white and nonwhite partisans, 25 points in the 1970s, widened to 34 points in the 1980s and has stayed there since.
- **Act II: Religion (1980s–2000s).** Beginning with Ronald Reagan's 1980 campaign, Republican strategists forged a durable alliance with conservative white evangelicals and the nascent Moral Majority, fusing free-market economics with traditionalist family and religious norms. Issues such as abortion, school prayer, and gay rights—which had previously cut across party lines—became partisan litmus tests, as Geoffrey Layman documents in [*The Great Divide*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-great-divide/9780231120586). In the 1970s, weekly churchgoers were just 5 percentage points more Republican than Americans who rarely or never attended services; today, that religious gap stands at 25 points.
- **Act III: Education (2000s–present).** In the 1970s, college graduates were a reliable pillar of the Republican coalition: partisans without a four-year degree were 11 percentage points *less* likely to identify as Republican than college graduates. Over the past two decades—and accelerating sharply after 2012—that relationship inverted. By 2021–24, partisans without a college degree were 14 points *more* Republican than college graduates (19 points more among white Americans). The bachelor's degree became one of the sharpest cultural and political fault lines in American life.
- **Act IV: Place (2010s–present).** As the knowledge economy concentrated credentialed professionals in dense metropolitan areas while manufacturing and resource economies hollowed out in the interior, urban and rural America drifted into separate political worlds. The partisan gap between residents of small towns and the countryside and those of cities over 50,000 nearly doubled, from 15 points in the 1970s to 29 points in 2021–24. Americans did not primarily [move to find partisan neighbors](https://doi.org/10.1086/687569), and movers' partisan leanings are [far too weak to produce today's map](https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2018.44); rather, [economic history](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/jonathan-a-rodden/why-cities-lose/9781541644274/) and cultural sorting turned population density itself into a political divide, leaving many Americans with [almost no exposure to the other party](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01066-z).

Figure 12 compares the ends of each divide (weekly churchgoers against those who rarely or never attend; small towns against cities), so its gaps are larger than those in Figure 10, which compares each group with everyone else.

By 2024, these four historical currents had combined into a remarkably tight social sorting, captured in the 60,000-respondent Cooperative Election Study.

![In 2024, church, college and place predict party. Bar charts from the 2024 Cooperative Election Study: the Republican share of partisans falls from 74% among those attending religious services more than weekly to 31% among those who never attend, from 55% among high-school graduates to 37% among postgraduates, and from 65% in rural areas to 35% in cities; dots show wider church and education gaps among white partisans.](polarization-divides-2024.png)

*Fig. 13 — 74% of partisans who attend religious services more than weekly are Republican, against 31% of those who never attend; 65% in rural areas against 35% in cities; 55% of high-school graduates against 37% of those with a postgraduate degree. Source: CES 2024 (n = 60,000); computed for this essay.*

Among all partisans in 2024, the Republican share falls step by step as religious attendance declines (from 74% among those attending more than weekly to 31% among those who never attend) and as places grow denser (from 65% in rural areas to 35% in cities), and it falls broadly with education (from 55% among high-school graduates to 37% among postgraduates). The religious and educational gradients are steeper still among white partisans (Figure 13). [Pew's study of validated voters in the 2024 presidential election](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/06/26/voting-patterns-in-the-2024-election/) confirms the pattern at the ballot box: Donald Trump carried non-college voters 56% to 42% and rural voters 69% to 29%, while Kamala Harris carried college graduates 57% to 41% and urban voters 65% to 33%.

### In Congress and on the issues

Inside Capitol Hill, this demographic sorting destroyed the cross-cutting faction that had once brokered legislative compromise: the Southern Democrats.

![The rotation in Congress: the swing faction disappeared. Line chart, 1931–2025, of the average DW-NOMINATE score of House Republicans, Northern Democrats and Southern Democrats: Southern Democrats sit between the parties until the 1970s, then move left, and in 2025 (−0.42) sit slightly left of Northern Democrats (−0.38).](polarization-southern-democrats.png)

*Fig. 14 — Southern Democrats moved left after the civil rights laws, fast after 1994, and are now indistinguishable from Northern Democrats. Source: Voteview; computed for this essay.*

For four decades after the New Deal, Southern Democrats occupied the ideological middle ground between conservative Republicans and liberal Northern Democrats, supplying the pivotal votes for bipartisan coalitions (Figure 14). As the Voting Rights Act enfranchised Black Southerners and conservative white voters switched to the GOP—culminating in the 1994 Republican takeover of Congress—the conservative Southern Democrat all but vanished, replaced either by conservative Republicans or by progressive Democrats representing majority-minority and urban districts. Today, Southern House Democrats (−0.42) sit slightly to the left of Northern Democrats (−0.38), facing a Republican conference at +0.52 across an empty center.

As the parties' demographic coalitions rotated, public policy attitudes rotated with them. Because the General Social Survey has asked the same questions for five decades, we can watch it happen, question by question (Figure 15).

![The parties always split on economics. Now they split on culture too. Four small line charts, 1974–2024, of the share of Democrats and of Republicans agreeing with a statement. On reducing income gaps and helping pay medical bills, the parties were about 20 points apart in the late 1970s and 41 to 47 points apart in 2016–24. On legal abortion for any reason and same-sex relations, they were within a few points until the late 1980s and 37 and 29 points apart in 2016–24.](polarization-rotation-measured.png)

*Fig. 15 — On economics, Democrats and Republicans were already about 20 points apart in the late 1970s; the gaps are now 41 to 47 points. On abortion and same-sex relations, the parties were within a few points of each other until the late 1980s; the gaps are now 37 and 29 points. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay.*

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the divide between Democrats and Republicans was overwhelmingly economic. Democrats were about 20 points more likely than Republicans to say the government should reduce income gaps or help people pay medical bills. On abortion, the parties were indistinguishable: Republicans were, if anything, slightly more likely to say it should be legal for any reason. By 2016–24, the economic gaps had roughly doubled, to 47 and 41 points, and cultural gaps had opened where there were almost none: 37 points on abortion and 29 on same-sex relations. Much of the widening since the 2000s came from Democrats moving left on both kinds of question. Averaging four economic and four cultural questions, the parties' cultural gap grew from a fifth of their economic gap in 1974–84 to three-fifths in 2016–24. Party competition is no longer mainly an argument over how much the government should tax and spend; it has become a conflict over moral order and group identity as well.

### Why stacked divides breed enemies

Generations of political sociologists have stressed that cross-cutting cleavages are the shock absorbers of pluralist democracy. When citizens clash over marginal tax rates or labor law but share a church pew, a neighborhood, or a class background, their political rivals remain recognizable human beings with whom they must cooperate in everyday life.

When social cleavages stack on top of one another, those shock absorbers vanish. When your political opponent differs from you not only on tax policy, but also in race, religious observance, educational credentials, and whether they live in a dense urban core or a rural township, every election feels like a referendum on whose way of life will prevail. As political scientist Lilliana Mason has shown in [her research](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12089) and in her book [*Uncivil Agreement*](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226524689.001.0001), the alignment of partisan, racial, religious, and cultural identities generates intense emotional hostility and moral outrage even when citizens' underlying policy views remain moderate. Splits over marginal tax rates can be compromised at the bargaining table; clashes over moral status and group belonging cannot.

Figures 12 and 13 show each divide on its own. The mechanism this essay proposes needs more: that the divides now line up *together*, so that a partisan's race, faith, schooling, and home all point toward the same party. That is what "stacking" means here: convergence in the traits' associations with party, not that the traits themselves occur together more often. The General Social Survey can test this directly. Score each partisan by how many of four Republican-leaning traits they have—white, attends religious services weekly, no four-year degree, lives in a small town or the countryside—and see how sharply party follows the count (Figure 16).

![The four divides now point toward the same party. Left: line chart of the Republican share of partisans by the number of four Republican-leaning traits they have, for the 1970s, 1990s, 2010s and 2021–24; the 1970s line is nearly flat, from 28% with one trait to 42% with four, while the 2021–24 line climbs from 14% with none to 87% with four. Right: the odds multiplier for each extra trait rises from 1.26 in the 1970s to 2.63 in 2021–24, with 95% intervals, clustered by survey sampling unit, that do not overlap.](polarization-stacking.png)

*Fig. 16 — In the 1970s, partisans with all four Republican-leaning traits were only modestly more Republican than those with one (42% against 28%). In 2021–24 the share runs from 14% with none to 87% with all four. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay.*

In the 1970s the four traits pulled in different directions—lacking a degree then went with being *less* Republican—so a partisan with all four was only 14 points more likely to be Republican than one with just one. In 2021–24 the difference is 59 points. In a model that also controls for age, sex, and living in the South, each additional trait multiplied the odds of being Republican by 1.26 in the 1970s (95% interval 1.18 to 1.34, allowing for the survey's clustered design) and by 2.63 in 2021–24 (2.39 to 2.90). Even if each trait is coded in whichever direction it pointed in the 1970s, the 1970s figure is only 1.52. The four traits' joint fit to party identification, measured by McFadden's pseudo-R², more than tripled (see the [Methods and Data appendix](/pubs/the-great-rotation/methods-and-data.pdf)). Race has mattered about equally since the 1980s; what changed is that religion, education, and place now push in the same direction as race. Three limits apply. The traits still predict party only modestly; demography is far from destiny. The traits do not co-occur more often than they used to—what changed is their alignment with party. And the General Social Survey does not measure partisan hostility, so the link from stacked identities to animosity rests on Mason's studies rather than on these data.

### Is party reshaping the people?

A skeptic could read all of this backward. Party loyalty can change the traits themselves. Partisans adjust their churchgoing to fit their politics, as [Michele Margolis](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226555812.001.0001) and [Stratos Patrikios](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-008-9053-1) show; the rise of Americans with no religion was partly a reaction against the religious right, as [Michael Hout and Claude Fischer](https://doi.org/10.2307/3088891) found; and [Patrick Egan](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12496) shows that partisans shift even identities such as ethnicity and sexual orientation to match their politics. If party shapes the traits, part of what looks like stacking is party loyalty measuring itself. Two checks address this.

![Not just party reshaping people, and not just new generations. Left: the odds multiplier for each extra Republican-leaning trait, by period, for the four adult traits (1.26 in the 1970s to 2.63 in 2021–24) and for four traits fixed in childhood (1.11 to 1.83). Right: the education gap in Republican share by birth cohort; Americans born in 1925–44 go from 18 points less Republican without a degree in the 1970s to 8 points more in 2021–24.](polarization-childhood-cohorts.png)

*Fig. 17 — Left: traits fixed in childhood now predict party too. Right: the same generations changed sides on education. Source: General Social Survey, weighted; computed for this essay.*

The first check uses only traits fixed in childhood: being white, being raised in a fundamentalist denomination, having a father without a four-year degree, and growing up in the countryside or a small town. Adult partisanship cannot have changed any of them. Rebuilt from these traits, the count stacks too. Each extra childhood trait multiplied the odds of being Republican by 1.11 in the 1970s (95% interval 1.02 to 1.21) and by 1.83 in 2021–24 (1.67 to 2.01). Having a father without a degree went from 18 points *less* Republican to 12 points more (Figure 10). So at least part of the alignment runs from social position to party. The childhood count stacks less steeply than the adult count (1.83 against 2.63), which leaves room for influence in both directions (Figure 17, left).

The second check asks who changed. The realignment could have come from new generations arriving with new loyalties (replacement), or from the same generations changing sides (conversion). Take Americans born between 1925 and 1957, who were adults in the 1970s and are still sampled in 2010–24. Among them, partisans without a degree went from 10 points less Republican than graduates to 3 points more. When each five-year birth cohort is allowed its own gap, about three-fifths (59%) of the change in the education gap since the 1970s happened within cohorts, and nearly all of the change in the religious gaps (85% to 87%) and the class gap (98%). The realignment ran mostly through generations that changed sides (Figure 17, right). These are generations, not the same individuals, and changes within a generation mix the effects of aging with those of the times. For religion, conversion could still mean that people changed their churchgoing to fit their party.

### The voters left in between

Yet compressing both economic and cultural conflict onto a single party line produced a major structural side effect: it left millions of Americans cross-pressured, holding views that neither party's coalition combines.

![The map in 2024: two corners full, two cross-pressured. Two heat maps of where Democratic and Republican partisans fall on economic and cultural policy scales in 2024: 89% of Democrats are left of center on both, 57% of Republicans are right on both, and 18% of Republicans are left on economics but right on culture.](polarization-map-2024.png)

*Fig. 18 — 89% of Democrats are left of center on both axes. Republicans are more mixed: 57% are right on both, and 18% are left on economics but right on culture. Source: CES 2024 (n = 59,923); computed for this essay.*

Plotting the nearly 60,000 respondents of the 2024 CES across eight economic and nine cultural policy questions shows how many are cross-pressured (Figure 18). Fully 89% of Democrats cluster in the lower-left quadrant—economically left of center and culturally open. On the Republican side, 57% occupy the orthodox conservative upper-right quadrant—economically right of center and culturally traditional. That leaves two off-diagonal quadrants whose combinations neither party's majority shares: the 9% of all adults in the lower-right quadrant (fiscally conservative, culturally liberal) and, still larger, the 11% of all adults—including 18% of rank-and-file Republicans—in the upper-left quadrant who favor left-of-center economic policies alongside traditional cultural norms. These shares depend on which questions are asked and where the line is drawn: across reasonable alternatives, between 8% and 15% of adults land in the upper-left corner. They describe views on these items; whether these voters feel unrepresented is a separate question the survey does not answer.

For decades, neither party leadership spoke for this upper-left populist bloc: national Democrats moved steadily toward cosmopolitan cultural liberalism, while establishment Republicans championed deregulation, free trade, and entitlement restraint. These cross-pressured voters became a decisive prize of twenty-first-century populist politics. In [Lee Drutman's survey of the 2016 electorate](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/did-economic-stress-or-cultural-anxiety-power-trumps-win), the voters who switched from Barack Obama in 2012 to Donald Trump in 2016 came overwhelmingly from this corner, though the exact shares depend on the questions used, as the [Niskanen Center notes](https://www.niskanencenter.org/libertarians-just-might-exist/).

## 4. Elites diverged, and the right moved further

Ordinary citizens did not sort themselves in a vacuum. The congressional record shows the parties' elites diverging from the late 1970s. Whether elites led or followed the public is harder to establish, because comparable surveys of partisan feeling begin only in 1978.

In congressional roll-call voting between 1971 and 2025, the average House Republican shifted from +0.25 on Voteview's first-dimension ideological scale to +0.52—a rightward move of 0.27 units. Over the same half-century, the average House Democrat shifted from −0.31 to −0.39—a leftward move of 0.08 units. On the floor of the House, the Republican conference moved more than three times as far from the center as the Democratic caucus. As Matt Grossmann and David Hopkins have documented in [*Asymmetric Politics*](https://academic.oup.com/book/6600), this asymmetry reflects a deep structural difference between the two coalitions: the modern Republican Party is organized as an ideological movement that prizes doctrinal purity, whereas the Democratic Party operates as a loose federation of social and constituency interest groups seeking concrete policy deliverables.

Part of the Republican movement came through turnover rather than conversion: since 1981, when a House seat passed from one Republican to another, the newcomer has usually voted further right than the member replaced (Section 7).

That legislative asymmetry raises a central puzzle: if congressional Republicans moved so much further from the center than congressional Democrats, why is emotional hostility in the public roughly symmetrical? Democrats and Republicans [dislike and dehumanize each other about equally](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2001263117).

Part of the explanation lies in how citizens form political judgments. Most voters do not derive policy positions from abstract ideological axioms; they take cues from the leaders of their political tribe. Clearer elite positions [revived mass partisanship](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055401003045); voters tend to [adopt their leaders' views rather than choose leaders by their views](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226472157.001.0001); and many self-described conservatives [follow Trump to liberal positions when told he holds them](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055418000795). When party elites polarize, partisans [lean even harder on party endorsements and discount substantive information](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055412000500). One plausible sequence follows. As Republican lawmakers moved sharply right on taxes, regulation, and immigration, Democratic voters saw an escalating threat to the social safety net and civil rights. As Democratic elites embraced rapid cultural change on race, gender, and secularism, conservative voters perceived an existential assault on traditional institutions. Each camp reacted to the other side's most visible moves, convincing both bases that their opponents had lost their minds.

Comparative evidence across advanced democracies is consistent with that account, though with ten to twelve countries it can only suggest it.

![Shared shocks do not predict which democracies polarized. Dot chart with 95% intervals of the correlation between each candidate driver's trend and the trend in affective polarization across 10 to 12 democracies: elite polarization +0.73, nonwhite share +0.59, 24-hour TV news +0.35, sorting +0.32, foreign-born share 0.00, trade −0.07, online news −0.19, internet use −0.48 and inequality −0.52; only elite polarization's interval excludes zero.](polarization-drivers.png)

*Fig. 19 — Correlation between the trend in each candidate driver and the trend in partisan hostility, across 10 to 12 democracies, with 95% intervals. Elite polarization shows the strongest association, but its interval is wide and it is not significant once nine tests are taken into account. Source: Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2024) replication data; computed for this essay.*

When we compare trends across the twelve OECD democracies in the Boxell, Gentzkow, and Shapiro dataset (Figure 19), polarization among party elites shows the strongest association with rising mass hostility: r = +0.73 across the ten countries with data, with a 95% interval from +0.19 to +0.93. It is the only interval that excludes zero, but the evidence is thinner than the point estimate suggests. With nine candidate drivers tested, the result is no longer significant after a standard correction for multiple comparisons (Holm-adjusted p = 0.15); it leans on the United States, without which the correlation falls to +0.50; the elite measure consists of three to five expert ratings per country between 1982 and 2003, and fitting each country's hostility trend to that same window lowers the correlation to +0.65; and a correlation across countries cannot say whether elites moved the public or the public moved its elites. Elite polarization is the strongest observed association, not proof that elites caused mass hostility. Growth in the nonwhite share of the population shows the next largest positive association (r = +0.59), followed by the proliferation of private 24-hour television news channels (r = +0.35) and mass partisan-ideological sorting (r = +0.32). By contrast, growth in the foreign-born population share shows no association (r = 0.00), while trade exposure (r = −0.07), online news consumption (r = −0.19), internet adoption (r = −0.48), and income inequality (r = −0.52) show no positive association; all of their intervals include zero.

Where political leaders fracture along sharp ideological lines or political entrepreneurs enter parliament to politicize cultural grievances, voters follow: [when a radical-right party entered the Dutch parliament in 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12449), voters polarized immediately and persistently. Across eleven democracies experiencing severe polarization, [Jennifer McCoy and Murat Somer](https://doi.org/10.1177/0002716218818782) identify political entrepreneurs who reorganize politics around an "us versus them" cleavage as the common driver. In the cases these studies examine, leaders divided first and the public followed.

## 5. Economic shocks: triggers, not engines

If political elites steered the realignment, what role did economic upheaval play? A vast literature points to rising income inequality, deindustrialization, and financial crises as the root causes of contemporary political anger.

The evidence suggests that economic shocks pack real electoral force but act more as *triggers* than as *engines*: rather than building new fault lines, they appear to activate those that identity sorting had already laid down.

Consider aggregate income inequality. In the United States, the pre-tax income share of the top 1% bottomed out at 10.4% in 1978 and [roughly doubled](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/income-share-top-1-before-tax-wid) over the next four decades, rising roughly as Congress pulled apart in what Nolan McCarty, Keith Poole, and Howard Rosenthal famously called the ["dance of ideology and unequal riches"](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262528627). Yet across our twelve-country sample (Figure 19), rising income inequality correlates *negatively* with changes in partisan hostility (r = −0.52, with an interval that includes zero). Sweden's inequality rose fastest of the twelve while its partisan hostility fell. Aggregate inequality cannot be the sufficient cause of American animus.

The voters' side of the ledger points the same way. If economic grievance were the engine of the realignment, income and class should divide the parties more sharply today than in the 1970s. They divide them less. A partisan's class identity and income now say almost nothing about their party, while their religion, education and hometown say a great deal (Section 3).

Localized trade shocks and acute financial crises show much cleaner causal effects precisely because voters interpret economic pain through the prism of social identity. When [David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson, and Kaveh Majlesi](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20170011) studied the "China shock"—the surge in Chinese import competition after 2000—they found that the hardest-hit districts "disproportionately removed moderate representatives." Crucially, however, the *direction* of that ideological swing depended on local racial demography: trade-shocked majority-white districts swung toward conservative Republicans, whereas trade-shocked majority-minority districts swung toward liberal Democrats. Similarly, [county-level manufacturing layoffs among white workers](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055421000022) boosted Donald Trump's vote share in 2016, while layoffs among nonwhite workers reduced it; in Western Europe, [import competition from China lifted nationalist parties](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12358).

Historical financial crises follow a similar script. Across 20 advanced economies and more than 800 elections since 1870, [far-right vote shares rose about 30%](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2016.03.006) in the five years after a systemic financial crisis, though most of the effect faded within a decade. As political scientist [Yotam Margalit has argued](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.33.4.152), acute economic insecurity can tip close elections without explaining much of the vote. Economic shocks supply the spark; stacked social cleavages appear to determine which way the explosion blows.

## 6. The container: two parties, winner take all

Why did the educational and cultural realignment that swept nearly every Western democracy over the past half-century produce an acute crisis of mutual hostility in the United States while leaving many European peers comparatively civil? The most plausible answer lies in the institutional container of American politics.

Popular reformers frequently blame two procedural bogeymen: partisan gerrymandering and closed primary elections. Yet decades of empirical political science indicate that neither accounts for the macro-trend. The United States Senate, which is never redistricted, polarized almost exactly as much as the House, and [simulations with neutral districts](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2009.00393.x) reproduce most of the House's polarization. Likewise, [historical](https://doi.org/10.1561/100.00008052) and [cross-state](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12070) studies find that primary rules have had little direct effect on the ideological extremity of elected lawmakers, and the [evidence on top-two primaries](https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2016.43) is mixed (see Appendix C).

The institutional feature this essay proposes as the best explanation is America's combination of single-member plurality districts and a rigid two-party system. Because that system is old, it cannot explain the timing on its own; the claim is that it interacts with the new sorting, which the comparative evidence suggests but does not test.

In comparative perspective, [majoritarian electoral systems](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108914123) tend to show more affective polarization than [proportional, consensus-oriented democracies](https://doi.org/10.1080/13510347.2022.2117300), although proportional representation is no vaccine: [several proportional systems in Southern and Central-Eastern Europe](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6765.12351) are more hostile than America's. Where multiple parties govern together in coalitions, voters express [warmer feelings toward parties that have served as coalition partners](https://doi.org/10.1177/00104140221100197) with their own. Across Western Europe, higher-educated professionals drifted left and high earners stayed right—the ["Brahmin Left" and "Merchant Right"](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab036) of Amory Gethin, Clara Martínez-Toledano, and Thomas Piketty—while working-class traditionalists drifted toward the nationalist right. Proportional electoral rules allowed [new parties to form](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2017.1310279) along [the new cultural axis of integration against demarcation](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511790720)—Green parties on the cosmopolitan left and populist radical-right parties on the nationalist right—while traditional center-left and center-right parties continued to bargain over economic management (see Appendix D).

In the United States, Duverger's law and winner-take-all elections locked out third parties, forcing the entire multidimensional realignment into the same two nineteenth-century vessels. Every cleavage—race, religion, education, geography, economics, and culture—was compressed onto a single binary line of combat: the ["two-party doom loop"](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190913854.001.0001) that Lee Drutman describes.

Two modern institutional developments plausibly made that binary compression more toxic:

1. **The Era of Insecure Majorities.** Between 1955 and 1995, Democrats held the House of Representatives for forty consecutive years, by an average margin of about 90 seats between 1955 and 1979. Because majority status was not seriously in doubt from one cycle to the next, minority Republicans had practical incentives to bargain with committee chairs to shape legislation, and majority Democrats could afford defections. Since 1995, House control has flipped five times, with majorities averaging about 30 seats. As political scientist Frances Lee has shown in [*Insecure Majorities*](https://lareviewofbooks.org/blog/interviews/partisan-conflict-separate-policy-talking-frances-lee/), when control of Congress hangs in the balance every two years, legislative compromise becomes strategically self-defeating: handing the majority party a bipartisan policy victory undermines the minority's campaign case for retaking power.
2. **The Collapse of Local News.** Between 2005 and 2025, [nearly 40% of American local newspapers disappeared](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2025/)—falling from 8,891 to 5,419—and newspapers [lost 56% of their newsroom jobs](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/07/13/u-s-newsroom-employment-has-fallen-26-since-2008/) between 2008 and 2020. When a community loses its local newspaper, voters lose the beat reporting needed to judge a member of Congress on local service or independent character. [Where a paper closed](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqy051), voters became more likely to vote the same party for president and Senate; [where voters got more in-state television coverage](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055420000829), they knew more about their governors and senators and split their tickets more. Without local information, local and congressional elections become referenda on the two national party brands.

![Old cleavages, stacked by two parties, amplified by media. Evidence map: dash-dot arrows mark links supported by causal evidence from other studies, dashed arrows links this essay proposes but does not test. Four old cleavages (race, religion, education, place) sort the parties; elite polarization, triggered by economic shocks and amplified by partisan media and misperceptions, leads voters to mass affective polarization inside a two-party, winner-take-all container; consequences such as safe seats and distrust feed back into the hostility.](polarization-layered-account.png)

*Fig. 20 — The layered account this essay proposes, drawn as an evidence map. Boxes summarize documented trends; arrows show which links rest on causal evidence from other studies and which are proposed here. The consequences, Sections 7–9, feed back into the hostility.*

Taken together, these forces form a layered account—an interpretation that ties the evidence together rather than a tested causal model (Figure 20): four historical cleavages sorted the electorate; polarizing elites diverged first in the record we have; a winner-take-all two-party container stacked every divide onto one axis; economic shocks triggered local realignments; and nationalized media amplified the conflict. Once assembled, this structure began reshaping the choices placed before voters.

## 7. Consequence one: the menu

The most damaging byproduct of the Great Rotation may not be that voters dislike one another; it may be that sorting has re-engineered the menu of candidates appearing on the ballot. Citizens can only vote for the candidates offered to them. Two claims need to be kept apart here. The first—that general elections have become far less competitive—is well documented. The second—that this machinery selects more factional nominees—is more inferential, and the evidence for it is partial.

![The machinery that turned sorting into safe seats. Four panels: districts splitting their presidential and House votes fall from 44% in 1972 and 1984 to 3.7% in 2020 and 2024; swing House seats fall from 164 in 1999 to 87 in 2025; 83% to 87% of House seats were settled in primaries in 2020–24, decided by 7% to 10% of eligible voters; newspaper newsroom jobs fall from 71,000 in 2008 to 31,000 in 2020.](polarization-machinery.png)

*Fig. 21 — Of the 77 swing seats lost between 1997 and 2023, the Cook Political Report attributes 45 to voters sorting themselves and 32 to redistricting. Sources: [Brookings Vital Statistics on Congress, Table 2-16](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Chapter-2-Full.pdf); [Cook Political Report](https://www.cookpolitical.com/cook-pvi/realignment-more-redistricting-has-decimated-swing-house-seats); [Unite America](https://www.uniteamerica.org/articles/analysis-87-of-u-s-house-elections-already-determined-in-primaries-by-just-7-of-americans); [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/07/13/u-s-newsroom-employment-has-fallen-26-since-2008/); [Medill](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/).*

Three interlocking shifts have hollowed out general-election accountability (Figure 21):

1. **Ticket-splitting and swing districts have nearly vanished.** In 1984, Ronald Reagan carried 49 states in his presidential landslide, yet Democrats kept firm control of the House because 44% of congressional districts voted for one party for president and the other for the House, most of them backing Reagan at the top of the ballot and a Democrat for Congress. By 2020 and 2024, only 16 of 435 House districts (3.7%) split their presidential and congressional outcomes. As straight-ticket voting hardened and geographic sorting deepened, the number of competitive House swing districts (those between D+5 and R+5 on the Cook Partisan Voting Index) fell by nearly half, from 164 in 1999 to 87 in 2025. Over a slightly different window, 1997 to 2023, [the Cook Political Report](https://www.cookpolitical.com/cook-pvi/realignment-more-redistricting-has-decimated-swing-house-seats) attributes 45 of the 77 swing seats lost to voters sorting themselves and 32 to redistricting.
2. **Low-turnout primaries have become the de facto election.** Because roughly five in six House districts are now safe for one party, the decisive election takes place not in November, but in the dominant party's primary. In 2024, [by Unite America's estimate](https://www.uniteamerica.org/articles/analysis-87-of-u-s-house-elections-already-determined-in-primaries-by-just-7-of-americans), 87% of U.S. House seats were effectively settled in primaries in which about 7% of eligible Americans voted. [Primary voters](https://pac.org/?p=62569) are not much more extreme on policy than their party's other voters, but they are more engaged and more drawn to insurgents out of disgust with the establishment.
3. **The November penalty for extremism has withered.** For decades, general elections punished ideological excess. [Andrew Hall found](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055414000641) that between 1980 and 2010, nominating an ideological extremist over a moderate in a competitive House primary cost a party roughly 9 to 13 percentage points of the November vote and cut its chance of winning the seat by 35 to 54 percentage points. That penalty has been shrinking: as [Brandice Canes-Wrone and Michael Kistner](https://doi.org/10.1561/100.00019222) put it, voters' accountability for candidates' positions "decreases gradually from 1980 through recent elections." Driven by negative partisanship, voters will hold their noses and back almost any nominee wearing their team's jersey rather than let the hated opposition win.

Does the machinery actually select different nominees? Roll-call records allow two descriptive checks. The first compares newcomers with everyone else in their party. Using scores estimated from each Congress's votes alone, first-term House Republicans voted to the right of continuing Republicans in 18 of the 22 Congresses from 1981 to 2023, while first-term Democrats voted closer to the center than continuing Democrats in 19 of the 22, plausibly because many Democratic newcomers win the party's most competitive seats. But that comparison mixes who gets nominated with which districts happen to change hands.

The second check holds the district fixed. It looks only at House seats that passed from one member to a newcomer of the same party at a regular election, within a single redistricting decade, and compares the newcomer's votes in their first Congress with the predecessor's votes in their last. It also allows for how far the party's continuing members moved over the same two years.

![Same seat, new member: Republican newcomers vote further right. Dot chart by era, 1949–2023, of how much further from the center House newcomers vote than the same-party members they replaced in the same district. Republican newcomers sat 0.04 closer to the center in 1949–69 and 0.035 to 0.047 further right in each era since 1981; Democratic newcomers stay within a few hundredths of their predecessors throughout.](polarization-same-seat.png)

*Fig. 22 — Positive values mean the newcomer votes further from the center than the predecessor did, beyond the drift of the party's continuing members. Source: Voteview Nokken–Poole scores; computed for this essay.*

Before 1970, Republican newcomers sat slightly closer to the center than the members they replaced. Since 1981, across 307 such seats, they have sat further right on average, by 0.04 on the roll-call scale of Figure 3 (95% interval 0.02 to 0.06), and did so in about two of every three seats (Figure 22). Democratic newcomers show no outward shift (−0.01, interval −0.04 to +0.01, across 194 seats). The difference is small for any one seat, but it adds up. By a rough count, these handovers account for about a quarter of the House Republicans' rightward move between 1979–80 and 2023–24. [Sean Theriault](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354068806064730) found that replacing members accounted for about two-thirds of congressional polarization after 1973, most of it from conservative Republicans taking seats from moderate Southern Democrats. The comparison here isolates a different channel: replacement within the same party and the same seat.

This is the strongest descriptive evidence in the essay for the claim that the machinery selects more factional nominees, and it mirrors the asymmetry in Section 4. It still does not show *why* the newcomers differ. Primary voters, activists and donors, national party recruitment, or a district electorate that changed between elections could each produce the pattern. Linking each seat to its primary results and to how safe it was would test the mechanism directly; that requires district election returns this essay did not obtain. Roll-call scores also cannot capture the combative style that wins attention.

The cast of characters dominating contemporary national politics illustrates the proposed mechanism, though it does not test it. Donald Trump now serves a second presidential term; Bernie Sanders has defined the ideological pole of the progressive movement for a decade; Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez sits in the top tier of the 2028 Democratic field and [led a May 2026 AtlasIntel poll](https://www.newsweek.com/aoc-surges-lead-2028-primary-first-time-poll-11941529), though most polls still put Kamala Harris ahead.

To note their shared structural origins is not to draw a false moral equivalence among them. Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez have pursued their agendas entirely within constitutional norms and the rules of electoral democracy; Donald Trump sought to overturn the 2020 presidential election and disrupted the peaceful transfer of power—a bright line that separates constitutional politics from regime subversion. Structurally, however, all three fit what a sorted, media-amplified system appears to reward: magnetic factional champions built to mobilize an energized base rather than broker compromises across the center.

## 8. Consequence two: distrust as a verdict

We can now return to the alarming Gallup finding with which we began: only 51% of Americans still trust the political judgment of their fellow citizens. This section proposes a plausible mechanism for that decline, not a demonstrated cause. It concerns a narrower measure than the interpersonal trust of Section 1: Gallup asks about confidence in the American people's judgment on political issues, not whether most people can be trusted. The 2026 survey interviewed about 1,000 adults by telephone between September 1 and 17, with a margin of error of about ±4 points, so a one- or two-point move in a single year is noise; a decline from the 70s to 51% is not.

![Trust in government now depends on who holds the White House. Line chart, 1958–2025, of trust in the federal government among Republicans and Democrats, which flips with the party of the president; in September 2025 Republicans stand at 26% and Democrats at 9%.](polarization-trust-seesaw.png)

*Fig. 23 — Between May 2024 and September 2025, Democrats' trust in the federal government fell from 35% to 9% while Republicans' rose from 11% to 26%. Source: [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/).*

Start with vertical trust in the state. Citizens no longer evaluate the federal government primarily by its administrative competence or policy performance; instead, institutional trust flips like a switch whenever the presidency changes hands (Figure 23). Between May 2024 and September 2025—as the White House passed from Joe Biden to Donald Trump—Democratic trust in the federal government collapsed from 35% to 9%, while Republican trust more than doubled from 11% to 26%. Part of that swing reflects real change—the new administration cut and reorganized the federal workforce in 2025—but the longer Pew series shows the same seesaw at every change of party in the White House, which points to partisan identity as well as performance. In a polarized two-party system, trust in government is hard to raise, because each election lowers it among the losers as it raises it among the winners.

Horizontal trust—confidence in the political judgment of the American people—may follow a similar dynamic, one step removed. This is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated feedback loop.

![Distrust of politicians came first; distrust of voters followed. Line chart of Gallup's trust in politicians and trust in the American people's political judgment: trust in politicians drops from 66% to 49% between 2008 and 2009, and trust in the people falls from 73% in 2009 to 51% in 2026.](polarization-people-vs-politicians.png)

*Fig. 24 — Trust in politicians fell from 66% to 49% between 2008 and 2009. Trust in the public's judgment, which had held in the 70s, fell every year from 2010 to 2016 and rose with trust in politicians in 2017. Source: Gallup Governance topline, September 2026.*

We never encounter "the American people" in the abstract; we encounter them through the politicians they put in office. [Gallup suggested this reading in 2013](https://news.gallup.com/poll/164678/political-trust-american-people-new-low.aspx): eroding faith in the public "may reflect the fact that it is the American people who elected those poorly regarded representatives." The sequence in Figure 24 is telling: public confidence in politicians plunged 17 points in a single year during the 2008–2009 financial crisis (from 66% to 49%). Confidence in the American people's judgment—which had been 70% or higher in every reading from 1974 to 2009—began a seven-year slide the very next year and has fallen in 13 of the 15 surveys since 2009, reaching 51% today. The chart cannot prove the link: year-to-year changes in the two lines are only weakly correlated (r = 0.12), so the evidence lies in the sequence and the turning points. It establishes an order of events, not a cause.

Strikingly, this disillusionment is bipartisan: in 2026, just 53% of Democrats, 51% of Republicans, and 49% of independents express trust in the public's political judgment. Looking out at the same country, each group sees a damning indictment of the electorate:

- **Democrats** look across the aisle and see tens of millions of fellow citizens twice electing a president who challenged the peaceful transfer of power and attacked constitutional checks and balances.
- **Republicans** look across the aisle and see tens of millions of voters endorsing progressive cultural orthodoxies and expansive state intervention that they regard as alien to American traditions.
- **Independents** look at both parties and see an electorate that repeatedly elevates cable-news pugilists and rewards legislative brinkmanship over basic governance.

When voters repeatedly confront constrained choices between antagonistic political coalitions, their declining confidence in the judgment of fellow citizens becomes understandable, even if that judgment rests on an incomplete picture of what those citizens actually believe—a picture Section 9 shows to be badly distorted.

## 9. How extreme politics keeps it going

If polarization were merely the passive residue of historical sorting, it might eventually subside as new issues cross-cut old coalitions. Instead, it persists because extreme politics has locked into a self-sustaining equilibrium: a polarized, fearful electorate is the safest environment in which a factional politician can operate.

![The polarization trap: a proposed mechanism. Cycle diagram with dashed arrows: shared shocks and the sorting of the parties feed a loop in which affective polarization leads to factional nominees, no November penalty, the caricature confirmed, and distrust as a verdict, which feeds back into affective polarization.](polarization-trap.png)

*Fig. 25 — A proposed self-sustaining loop. Each step is documented in part; the loop as a whole is a hypothesis.*

Three proposed mechanisms could keep this feedback loop spinning (Figure 25):

1. **Existential fear buys unconditional loyalty.** Once a leader convinces supporters that the opposing party represents a mortal threat to democracy, faith, or national survival, accountability within the coalition evaporates. Voters who believe the republic will perish if the other side wins cannot afford to punish their own standard-bearer for corruption, incompetence, or constitutional norm-breaking. As [Kenneth Roberts](https://doi.org/10.1017/gov.2021.14) notes, populist movements often arise in societies that are not yet deeply divided and then divide them, and [in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and Venezuela](https://doi.org/10.1177/0002716218818782) polarizing leaders built entrenched camps on older rifts, binding supporters to the leader through fear of the other side. For politicians whose careers rest on negative partisanship, bipartisan compromise is not a badge of honor—it is disarmament in the face of the enemy.
2. **Institutional referees are turned into combatants.** In an all-or-nothing struggle between stacked identities, no institution is permitted to remain neutral. Courts, election administrators, statistical agencies, universities, and criminal investigators are increasingly judged by whether their decisions help or hurt the tribe. Adverse rulings or inconvenient facts are dismissed as proof that the referees are rigged, while razor-thin congressional majorities make legislative obstruction rational. As gridlock festers and public problems go unsolved, institutional cynicism deepens, making anti-system rhetoric even more appealing.
3. **Each side's loudest champions confirm the other side's worst caricatures.** Partisans do not fight their actual neighbors; they fight a grotesquely distorted mental image of the opposing party.

![Each side fights a caricature of the other. Bar charts: Republicans estimate that 56% of Democrats hold extreme views against an actual 29%, and Democrats estimate 53% of Republicans against an actual 34%; the gap rises from 8 points among those who hardly follow the news to 28 points among those who follow it most of the time.](polarization-perception-gap.png)

*Fig. 26 — Partisans estimate that 55% of the other side holds extreme views; about 30% do. The most attentive news consumers are the most wrong. Source: [More in Common, The Perception Gap (2019)](https://perceptiongap.us/).*

Surveys reveal how badly partisans misread one another. [Americans overestimate](https://doi.org/10.1086/697253) the share of party-stereotypical groups in the opposing party—such as the share of Democrats who are gay, lesbian, or bisexual or atheist, or the share of Republicans who earn over $250,000 a year—by about 342% on average; they think 38% of Republicans earn more than $250,000, against an actual 2.2%. Similarly, partisans estimate that more than half of the opposing party (56% in Republicans' view of Democrats; 53% in Democrats' view of Republicans) holds extreme policy views, when the true proportion is roughly 30%, [according to More in Common](https://perceptiongap.us/) (Figure 26). Strikingly, the perception gap is more than three times wider among citizens who follow the news "most of the time" (28 points) than among those who hardly ever follow it (8 points).

Here, on this account, the feedback loop closes. Because safe-seat primaries and outrage-driven media ecosystems elevate theatrical bomb-throwers, the politicians who dominate cable chyrons and social feeds look exactly like the caricatures the other side fears. Each party's base nominates a fighter; the opposing party points to that fighter as Exhibit A of the enemy's depravity; and both electorates conclude that their neighbors across the aisle cannot be trusted.

Crucially, however, ordinary partisan dislike does not automatically translate into an appetite for political violence or authoritarian rule. In a landmark megastudy testing 25 interventions across 32,059 Americans, [Jan Voelkel and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh4764) found that while 23 treatments reduced partisan animosity, only six reduced support for undemocratic practices and only five reduced support for partisan violence. What *does* substantially reduce willingness to endorse political violence is correcting false *metaperceptions*: showing partisans that the overwhelming majority of the opposing party actually rejects violence cut support for partisan violence by 34% in [one study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2116851119). Much of the danger comes less from our policy disagreements than from the phantom monsters our political and media machinery projects onto the wall.

### Other readings, and what would tell them apart

The account in this essay is one way to connect the evidence. The companion paper, [*Alternative Hypotheses and Analyses*](/pubs/the-great-rotation/alternative-hypotheses.pdf), describes two others that fit about as well.

- **Elites and activists first.** Party activists and officeholders, especially on the right, moved first for their own reasons: an ideological movement, insecure majorities, the end of the Southern Democrats. Voters followed their cues, and the social sorting of the 2000s and 2010s was a consequence of party polarization rather than its root. The same-seat comparison in Section 7 fits this reading at least as well as it fits this essay's.
- **Media-accelerated sorting.** The realignment began with race and religion, as this essay says, but cable news after the mid-1990s and online platforms after 2010 sped it up. Media cannot be the origin of trends that began in the 1970s, but most of the rise in partisan dislike came after 1996, when they could have mattered.

The data assembled here cannot choose among the three. Three kinds of evidence could:

1. **Panels that follow the same people for years**, measuring their identities, issue positions and feelings toward the other party. If stacked identities breed hostility, hostility should rise after a person's identities line up with their party, not before. The American National Election Studies measure identities and hostility in the same respondents; their microdata require registration and were not used here.
2. **Differences in timing across places.** If media accelerated the rise, places that got cable news, broadband or platforms earlier should show faster growth in hostility afterward, compared with similar places that got them later.
3. **District-level links.** If safe seats select factional nominees, the outward shift in Section 7 should be larger where seats are safer and primaries more contested, and distrust should grow more where voters faced such nominees. That requires district election and primary returns.

## 10. What would help

If American polarization is rooted in structural sorting inside a winner-take-all duopoly, as this essay proposes, it becomes clear why well-meaning pleas for "civility" fall flat. A [recent meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2508827122) of 77 experimental depolarization interventions found that they raise warmth toward the other party by an average of just 5 points on a 101-point scale—and three-quarters of that modest gain evaporates within a single week. Nor should reformers pine for the muddled mid-century party system: in 1950, the American Political Science Association [famously complained](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-009-9094-0) that the two parties were too ideologically indistinct to offer voters coherent governing accountability—and that mid-century bipartisanship rested in no small part on a tacit agreement to keep Jim Crow off the national agenda.

The goal of democratic reform is not to erase principled disagreement, but to unstack our cleavages so that citizens who disagree on taxes or trade are not automatically locked into total war over identity, faith, and geography. If this account is right, breaking the polarization trap means changing the incentives of the institutional container:

1. **Correct false metaperceptions at scale.** Because exaggerated beliefs about the other side's extremism and support for violence fuel anti-democratic attitudes, civic organizations, educators, and media outlets should deploy tested informational interventions—[correcting misperceptions of who belongs to the other party](https://doi.org/10.1086/697253) and [of how much it supports violence](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2116851119)—that expose partisans to the actual, far more moderate and nonviolent, distribution of views among their opponents. In [the largest test so far](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh4764) and elsewhere, their effects have been tracked for weeks, not years, so they would need to be repeated.
2. **Rebuild local journalism.** Restoring local civic information is essential to de-nationalizing elections. Philanthropic endowments, public-interest tax credits, and nonprofit newsroom models that put full-time reporters back on city hall and congressional beats give voters concrete, local criteria to reward independent-minded representatives and punish performative grandstanding; the evidence on [newspaper closures](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqy051) and [local television coverage](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055420000829) suggests that such information changes how people vote.
3. **Open the two-party container to multiparty competition.** Breaking the binary vice requires lowering the barriers that trap every societal cleavage inside two parties. At the state level, reviving *fusion voting*—allowing minor parties to cross-endorse major-party nominees—lets cross-pressured blocs (such as economically populist, culturally traditional voters or fiscally moderate, socially liberal voters) signal their distinct strength without spoiling elections. At the federal level, replacing single-member winner-take-all House districts with *proportional, multimember districts* (as proposed in the Fair Representation Act) could allow more coherent parties to compete and form shifting legislative coalitions across different issue dimensions, as peer democracies do, the case Lee Drutman makes in [*Breaking the Two-Party Doom Loop*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190913854.001.0001). Consensus institutions are associated with lower polarization, though that evidence is correlational.
4. **Ease the low-turnout primary bottleneck.** Where single-member districts remain, replacing closed partisan primaries with nonpartisan top-four or top-five primaries paired with ranked-choice voting in the general election—the model adopted in Alaska—is designed to make winners assemble a broad majority in November rather than cater to the 7% of voters who show up in a partisan primary. The evidence is mixed: [one study of California's top-two primary](https://doi.org/10.1561/113.00000012) finds more moderate legislators, [another](https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2016.43) no gain in representation. Alaska's system survived a repeal vote in 2024 by fewer than a thousand votes and faces another on November 3.
5. **Deliver tangible governing competence.** Nothing defangs demagogues faster than a state that visibly solves problems. When governments lower housing costs, maintain safe streets, manage borders humanely and in an orderly way, and deliver reliable public services, they drain the reservoirs of material grievance that factional entrepreneurs exploit.
6. **Enforce a non-negotiable constitutional boundary.** Finally, healthy multiparty competition requires absolute clarity about the rules of the game. Commitment to the rule of law, free and fair elections, the peaceful transfer of power, and the constitutional legitimacy of political opposition cannot be treated as just another culture-war bargaining chip.

## 11. Conclusion: change what they are offered

American democracy did not arrive at this precipice because citizens suddenly lost their capacity for self-government, nor because smartphone algorithms hijacked an otherwise harmonious republic. Over five decades, the foundational fault lines of American society—race, religion, education, and geography—rotated out of their old cross-cutting patterns and lined up behind two parties, while economic class, the divide that once organized party competition, stopped predicting party at all; that much the data show. The account proposed here adds that political elites, the Republican Party most of all, diverged first in the record we have, and that America's winner-take-all two-party system acted as a vice, compressing every economic, cultural, and demographic dispute into a single zero-sum contest. Those links are supported in part and remain to be tested.

If this account is right, the machinery sustains itself. Geographic and demographic sorting turns general-election districts into safe seats; safe seats hand candidate selection to tiny primary electorates; negative partisanship shields factional nominees from paying a price in November; and ordinary citizens look at the leaders the other half of the country elects and conclude that their fellow Americans can no longer be trusted.

Liberal democracy remains the finest political invention in human history—not because majorities are always wise, but because constitutional checks, minority protections, and regular elections allow a free people to correct their mistakes without bloodshed. Yet defending self-government does not mean defending an electoral architecture that systematically rewards division and leaves millions of voters cross-pressured.

The remedy is not to lecture Americans for losing faith in the choices currently placed before them. It is to reform the container so that it produces choices worthy of their trust. Unstack the divides, give cross-pressured voters a voice, and restore incentives to compete for broad majorities. That would not guarantee a return of public trust. But it could finally give Americans better reasons to trust one another.

## Appendix A: Dimensions of Political Division

Scholars and commentators routinely conflate four distinct phenomena under the single label of "polarization." Separating them is essential for diagnosing cause and effect.

| Dimension | What it measures | Empirical record in the United States |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ideological Polarization** | The ideological distance and dispersion of ordinary citizens' policy views | The share of Americans holding consistently liberal or conservative views across issue domains rose from 10% to 21% between 1994 and 2014, [according to Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2014/06/12/political-polarization-in-the-american-public/), while the share identifying as moderate in the GSS held steady (40% in 1974 vs. 37% in 2024). |
| **Partisan Sorting** | The degree to which social identities and ideological views align with party identification | In the [General Social Survey](https://gss.norc.org/), the Pearson correlation between 7-point party identification and 7-point ideological self-placement more than tripled from 0.17 in 1974 to 0.63 in 2024. |
| **Affective Polarization** | The gap between emotional warmth toward one's own party and dislike, distrust, or contempt toward the opposing party | The gap between in-party and out-party [ANES feeling thermometer ratings](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/E8Q4BX) (0–100) more than doubled from 27 points in 1978 to 56 points in 2020, 98% of it from cooling toward the out-party. |
| **Elite Polarization** | The ideological voting distance between Democratic and Republican lawmakers | The distance between party means on the DW-NOMINATE first dimension rose from 0.57 in 1977 to 0.91 in 2025, driven primarily by a rightward shift among congressional Republicans. In [Voteview](https://voteview.com/data) data, the parties last overlapped in the House in 2001–02 and in the Senate in 2003–04. |

### The Fiorina–Abramowitz Scholarly Debate

This four-part taxonomy resolves a classic debate in American political science. In their 2005 book [*Culture War? The Myth of a Polarized America*](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/004766434), Morris Fiorina, Samuel Abrams, and Jeremy Pope argued that mass polarization was largely a mirage: most Americans remained centrist, ambivalent, and pragmatic on policy, even as polarizing elites forced them to choose between extreme menu options. Responding in 2008 with ["Is Polarization a Myth?"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022381608080493), Alan Abramowitz and Kyle Saunders argued that politically engaged citizens had divided sharply along ideological and partisan lines.

Both camps captured an essential piece of the empirical reality:

- **Fiorina was right about policy distributions:** Ordinary Americans did not rush en masse to the ideological poles; on most individual policy questions, public opinion remains unimodal and clustered near the center.
- **Abramowitz was right about sorting and antipathy:** The alignment among party label, ideology, and demographic identity has tightened dramatically, and among engaged partisans, tribal loyalty and out-party hostility have reached modern highs.

### Methodological Debates on Cable News Effects

[Early quasi-experimental research by Stefano DellaVigna and Ethan Kaplan](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.122.3.1187) exploited the staggered local rollout of Fox News to identify a 0.4 to 0.7 percentage-point shift toward Republican presidential candidates in 2000; [Elliott Ash and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2024.105256) find effects of at least half a point in recent elections. A widely cited estimate derived from the structural model of [Gregory Martin and Ali Yurukoglu](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160812), who used channel position as an instrument for viewing, holds that Fox News added 6.3 percentage points to the Republican presidential vote share in 2008.

As statistician and political scientist [Andrew Gelman pointed out](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2019/01/24/no-i-dont-buy-that-claim-that-fox-news-is-shifting-the-vote-by-6-percentage-points/), however, that figure rests on strong modeling assumptions and on extrapolating from small local differences in channel position to the effect of the whole channel. Crucially, Martin and Yurukoglu themselves find that cable news can plausibly account for the public's modest rise in ideological polarization, but nowhere near the far faster polarization of elected representatives. Cable television's clearest documented effect on elites ran through politicians: [House members whose districts got Fox became less supportive of President Clinton](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022381614000425). Cable news amplified a divergence it did not start.

### Foreign Disinformation and Algorithmic Manipulation

Popular accounts of the 2016 presidential election attributed sweeping persuasive power to foreign social media operations, notably Russia's Internet Research Agency (IRA). Subsequent panel studies linking survey respondents directly to their Twitter feeds tell a much more sobering story.

Analyzing longitudinal survey data linked to respondents' Twitter feeds in 2016, [Gregory Eady and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-35576-9) established three core facts:

1. **Extreme concentration:** Just 1% of users accounted for 70% of all exposures to Russian IRA posts.
2. **Self-selection:** The users most exposed to foreign disinformation were overwhelmingly highly engaged partisans already firmly aligned with their party's nominee.
3. **No measurable persuasive impact:** Controlling for prior attitudes, the authors found no measurable relationship between exposure to Russian accounts and changes in attitudes, polarization, or voting behavior.

Foreign influence operations parasitized pre-existing partisan divisions; they did not create them.

## Appendix C: Institutional Hypotheses: Gerrymandering and Primary Rules

Two of the most frequently cited institutional explanations for congressional dysfunction—partisan gerrymandering and closed primary rules—fail to account for the historical trajectory of polarization, even though safe seats and low-turnout primaries now play a major role in *sustaining* it.

### Gerrymandering

The gerrymandering thesis holds that partisan mapmakers draw artificially safe districts that insulate incumbents from general-election competition and push lawmakers toward the ideological extremes. Three empirical findings tell against gerrymandering as the primary cause of congressional divergence:

1. **The Senate Control Test:** The United States Senate, whose constituencies are whole states and are never redistricted, polarized almost exactly as much as the House: the distance between the party means rose from 0.59 in 1977 to 0.92 in 2025, against 0.57 to 0.91 in the House, by this essay's calculations from [Voteview](https://voteview.com/data) data.
2. **Neutral Map Simulations:** [McCarty, Poole, and Rosenthal](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2009.00393.x) compared actual districts with simulated neutral ones and found that the simulations reproduce most of the House's polarization.
3. **Geographic Sorting Over Mapmaking:** As [Jonathan Rodden](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/jonathan-a-rodden/why-cities-lose/9781541644274/) and the [Cook Political Report](https://www.cookpolitical.com/cook-pvi/realignment-more-redistricting-has-decimated-swing-house-seats) have documented, the concentration of Democratic voters inside high-density urban cores and Republican voters across rural and exurban territory naturally generates lopsided districts even under neutral redistricting, and voter sorting has cost more swing seats than redistricting.

### Primary Election Systems

A parallel argument blames closed party primaries—which bar independent voters from participating—for empowering ideological purists. Yet historical comparisons across states and decades show that changing primary rules has had little standalone impact on legislative roll-call scores:

- **Historical Primary Competition:** [Shigeo Hirano, James Snyder, Stephen Ansolabehere, and John Mark Hansen](https://doi.org/10.1561/100.00008052) studied congressional primaries since their introduction early in the twentieth century and found little evidence that the introduction of primaries, primary turnout, or the threat of a primary challenge is associated with partisan roll-call voting.
- **Cross-State Primary Reforms:** [Eric McGhee, Seth Masket, Boris Shor, Steven Rogers, and Nolan McCarty](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12070) compared closed, semi-closed, open, and top-two primary systems across state legislatures and concluded that primary openness has "little, if any, effect on the extremism of the politicians it produces."

Why, then, do primaries matter so much today? Because once geographic sorting and straight-ticket voting made most House seats safe—in 2024, [by Unite America's estimate](https://www.uniteamerica.org/articles/analysis-87-of-u-s-house-elections-already-determined-in-primaries-by-just-7-of-americans), 87% were effectively decided in primaries—the primary became the *only* contested election left, and the [shrinking November penalty for extremism](https://doi.org/10.1561/100.00019222) weakened the general-election check that [used to restrain primary voters](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055414000641). Primary reform is likeliest to help when paired with general-election mechanisms—such as top-four or top-five nonpartisan primaries with ranked-choice voting, or proportional multimember districts—that restore genuine competition in November.

## Appendix D: Comparative Cleavage Theory

What this essay calls "The Great Rotation" is the American expression of a structural realignment documented across advanced post-industrial democracies.

### The "Brahmin Left" and "Merchant Right"

Compiling seven decades of post-election surveys across 21 Western democracies (1948–2020), economists [Amory Gethin, Clara Martínez-Toledano, and Thomas Piketty](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab036) uncovered a near-universal rotation in the social bases of party politics:

- **The Mid-Century Class System (1950s–1960s):** Party systems were organized along a single socioeconomic class axis. Both high-income earners and university-educated voters supported center-right parties, while lower-income and less-educated working-class voters supported socialist, labor, or social democratic parties (and in the US, the New Deal Democrats).
- **The Modern Multi-Elite System (2010s–2020s):** The link between income and education split into two distinct elites. University-educated professionals migrated steadily toward parties of the left (forming a credentialed "Brahmin Left"), while high-income business and financial elites remained with parties of the right (the "Merchant Right"). Simultaneously, non-college working-class voters drifted away from traditional left parties, many toward nationalist and right-wing populist parties.

### Integration versus Demarcation

Political scientists [Hanspeter Kriesi and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511790720) and [Liesbet Hooghe and Gary Marks](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2017.1310279) conceptualize this shift as a transnational cleavage pitting the winners of post-industrial globalization against its cultural and economic losers:

- **The Integration / Cosmopolitan Pole:** Credentialed urban professionals who thrive in the knowledge economy and favor international integration, open immigration, climate transition, and cultural pluralism.
- **The Demarcation / Nationalist Pole:** Non-college workers and residents of industrial and rural communities who face economic precarity from global trade and view rapid demographic and cultural change as a threat to national sovereignty and traditional status orders.

### The American Version

The General Social Survey shows the same rotation in the United States (Section 3). In the 1970s, race and class did nearly all the work of predicting a partisan's party, in roughly equal measure; by 2021–24, race and religion did, and class contributed almost nothing. Partisans without a four-year degree went from 11 points less Republican than graduates to 14 points more. The top third of earners stayed modestly more Republican until about 2014 (11 to 17 points) and is now barely different from everyone else (3 points), so the American "merchant right" has weakened along with the class divide as a whole.

### Why the United States Diverged in Affective Hostility

Although the educational realignment occurred across nearly all 21 Western democracies, affective hostility rose fastest in the United States and fell in several of its peers. In Western Europe's proportional electoral systems, the new cultural axis was absorbed through *party system differentiation*:

- **Green and Left-Libertarian parties** rose to represent the cosmopolitan, university-educated left.
- **Populist Radical-Right parties** rose to represent the culturally traditional, working-class right.
- **Mainstream Social Democratic, Conservative, and Christian Democratic parties** continued to compete and form governing coalitions around economic management.

Because proportional parliaments require multiparty coalitions to govern, European parties routinely bargain across ideological lines, and partisans [stay warmer toward parties that have governed alongside their own](https://doi.org/10.1177/00104140221100197). In the United States, a winner-take-all two-party system forced both the economic and cultural dimensions into a single head-on collision.
