"Is AI a bubble?" may be the most asked question in finance this year. It rarely gets a straight answer, because "bubble" means different things to different people. Some mean that stock prices are too high. Some mean that companies are building more than anyone will use. Others mean that too much of it runs on borrowed money.

It also helps to remember that a technology can be real and still be a bubble. Britain's railways in the 1840s and America's fiber-optic networks in the late 1990s changed their economies for good. Much of the money that built them was still lost. So the useful question is not whether AI works. It clearly does. The question is whether the trillions being spent on it will earn their keep.

In [my previous post](https://www.gokhanmergen.com/blog-what-is-a-bubble.html), I looked at prices: the Nasdaq is overvalued against its 55-year trend, but nowhere near the mania of 2000. Here I look under the hood, at the money going in and the money coming out. The numbers come from a longer analysis I finished at the end of October 2026, built mostly from company filings.

## Spending has outrun revenue

Start with what the AI build-out is for. The chips and data centers that Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle and a handful of newer players are buying serve three businesses: Google's and Meta's advertising (their ranking and recommendation systems run on the same AI hardware), cloud computing, and the AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic. Together that revenue comes to about $1.06 trillion in 2026.

Spending on AI and cloud infrastructure will reach about $910 billion this year. That is 86 cents spent for every dollar of revenue it serves. From 2019 to 2023, it was about 25 cents.

Since 2022, spending has grown about 64% a year and revenue about 21%. In my base scenario, which follows company guidance and analyst forecasts, spending peaks at 94% of revenue in 2027 before easing off.

A high ratio does not by itself prove overspending. Railways and power grids also cost a lot up front. What matters is whether each dollar eventually earns more than it costs.

## Is the money earning its keep?

Investors expect a return of roughly 9% a year on this kind of investment; below that, they would have been better off putting the money elsewhere. So the test is simple to state: does the AI build-out earn more than 9%?

**Looking back, it depends on how much credit AI gets.** From 2022 to 2026, operating profit across advertising, cloud and the AI labs rose by about $202 billion. Credit all of that to the build-out, and it earns 13–27% on the money invested, comfortably above 9%. But some of that growth would have happened anyway. Under some plausible assumptions about growth without AI, the return falls below 9%. Count only generative AI products, such as chatbots and coding assistants, and it is 2–6%.

**Looking forward, the bar keeps rising.** For all the equipment installed since 2019 to earn 9%, revenue must grow at least 22% a year until 2030. That is roughly its pace of the past four years, so it is possible. But the cushion is shrinking. Today, revenue covers what the equipment needs to earn about 2.5 times over. By 2030, in my base scenario, it covers it only 1.1 times. It is like a household whose income is growing nicely, but whose loan payments are growing faster.

The narrowest test is the starkest. Generative AI's own customer revenue will be roughly $150–190 billion in 2026, against roughly $950 billion of AI spending. Every dollar of AI capacity needs to earn about 21 cents a year to pay for itself. Today it earns roughly 5–7 cents, though much of the newest capacity is still ramping up.

## From cash to credit

Until recently, the best argument against an AI bubble was that the big tech companies paid for all of this out of their own pockets. Unlike the telecom companies of 2000, they were not borrowing to build.

That stopped being true in 2026. In 2024, the five biggest spenders generated $478 billion of cash from their businesses and spent $239 billion on equipment. By the third quarter of 2026, their quarterly spending of about $230 billion exceeded the roughly $199 billion of cash their businesses brought in. Alphabet, Meta and Amazon all reported negative free cash flow. Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle sold more than $228 billion of bonds in 2026 by mid-October, more than double the year before.

This matters because it changes who carries the risk. When a company spends its own cash and the bet disappoints, its shareholders earn less. When it borrows, bondholders, private-credit funds and the banks behind them are exposed too. Some of the commitments do not even appear in the reported figures: Meta's giant Hyperion data center, for example, sits in a separate vehicle largely owned and financed by an outside investor.

The big five can carry this debt. The trouble is at the edges. Oracle, the most stretched of the large spenders, is about 62% below its 2025 peak, and its debt is rated one notch above junk. CoreWeave, which rents out AI chips, is down 61% in a year, and at its October peak the cost of insuring its debt implied roughly a 60% chance of default within five years. Both borrowed heavily to serve AI labs that are themselves losing money.

## Why this is not 2000

The bulls have the stronger argument on demand. In a classic overbuilding bubble, capacity sits idle and prices collapse while construction continues. In 2002, by one widely cited estimate, only about 2.7% of America's fiber was in use.

Today, the opposite is true. SK hynix says its memory chips are sold out through 2029. Microsoft trimmed its spending plans because it could not get enough chips delivered. Rental prices for older Nvidia chips are about 40% above their low of late 2025. And the AI labs' revenue is real and astonishingly fast-growing: OpenAI and Anthropic together now bring in more than $115 billion a year at their current pace, up from under $10 billion at the start of 2025. Nothing in the dot-com era grew this fast at this scale.

Valuations of the leaders also look nothing like 2000. The biggest AI companies trade at 17–25 times next year's expected earnings. Nvidia, the Cisco of this cycle, trades at about 26 times its past year's earnings; at the peak of the dot-com bubble, Cisco traded at about 196.

Even the sharpest sceptics' best points look modest at the core. Michael Burry argues that tech companies flatter their profits by assuming their chips last six years when they may last four. He has a point, but redoing the math knocks only about 9–10% off the big companies' earnings. For thinly capitalized chip-rental firms, the same adjustment can wipe out their equity.

## The circle at the center

The bulls' blind spot is the money flowing in a circle. Suppliers increasingly fund their own customers. Nvidia has invested about $50 billion in AI labs, which buy its chips through the cloud companies. Two of the three largest investors in OpenAI's February funding round, Amazon and Nvidia, are also its suppliers. And about 40% of the $2.5 trillion of future contracts the four big cloud companies report is owed by just two customers, OpenAI and Anthropic.

Anthropic turned an operating profit in the second quarter of 2026. OpenAI plans to burn about $220 billion of cash before turning positive in 2030. This loop holds as long as the labs can keep raising money, which now depends on how investors receive their planned stock-market listings. It is the pattern of Lucent and Nortel in 1999, on a larger scale and with stronger companies behind it.

## The verdict: a bubble in layers

So, is AI a bubble? It is not one bubble but several layers:

- **At the core**, real demand that still exceeds supply.
- **Around it**, a spending cycle that may build more than it can earn.
- **At the edges**, a credit and speculative bubble that is already deflating.
- **Underneath**, a stock market so concentrated in a few companies that a problem in one sector could become a problem for the whole economy.

Scored against the common meanings of "bubble":

| What people mean by "bubble" | Is AI one? |
| --- | --- |
| Debt that only the boom can repay | **Yes, at the edges:** chip-rental firms, Oracle, data-center project debt |
| Building more than will earn its cost | **At risk:** no glut yet, but the plan needs fast growth to 2030 |
| Prices above what future profits justify | **Partly:** private AI labs and the index as a whole, not the big companies' earnings multiples |
| Buying in hope of selling to someone else | **Partly:** private funding rounds and new AI stock listings |
| Suppliers funding their own customers | **Partly:** Nvidia, Oracle and Microsoft's order books |
| A story driving prices more than results | **Partly:** promises of superintelligence underpin private valuations |
| Expectations running ahead of delivery | **Partly:** revenue beat expectations; business productivity gains so far have not |
| An economy leaning on one theme | **A risk:** the ten largest companies are about 38% of the S&P 500 |

The best historical comparison is not tulips or Pets.com, where the underlying asset was close to worthless. It is the railways and fiber: the technology is real and will be used, but investors who fund the late stages of the build can lose money while everyone else benefits. Two things still make AI different from 2000. The core spenders are among the most profitable companies in history, and the capacity they have built is fully used. Both could change in 2027–29, when the capacity now under construction comes online.

## What to watch

Three signposts will tell us which way this is heading:

1. **Spending plans for 2027**, announced in January and February. Raising them while free cash flow stays negative would confirm the drift from cash to credit.
2. **Revenue growth in 2027.** Growth above about 30% would support the bulls. Growth falling back toward its 2022–26 pace would leave revenue short of what the equipment needs to earn by 2030.
3. **The OpenAI and Anthropic stock listings.** The labs sit at the center of the financing loop. How the public market prices them will decide whether money keeps flowing to their suppliers.

If a crash does come, my bet is that it starts at the edges, in the borrowers and the private valuations, not in the technology. And when it does, someone will say AI was a bubble all along. The more accurate answer will be: parts of it were.
