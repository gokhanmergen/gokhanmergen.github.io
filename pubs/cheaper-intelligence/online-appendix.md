## Online Appendix A: Derivations

The results in the main text are standard (Hicks 1932; Allen 1938; Hamermesh 1986, 1993). They are derived here in the article's notation, together with the extensions used in the sections on measurement and on junior workers.

*Setup.* Let C($ω_{L}$, $ω_{M}$) be the unit cost function of a constant-returns technology, defined over the effective input prices $ω_{L}$ = w/$a_{L}$ and $ω_{M}$ = $p_{M}$/$a_{M}$, with partial derivatives $C_{1}$, $C_{2}$, $C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, and $C_{22}$. By Shephard's lemma, the input of effective labor per unit of output is $C_{1}$, so employment is

$$
L = \frac{Q \, C_1(\omega_L, \omega_M)}{a_L}. \tag{A1}
$$

Competitive pricing gives p = C($ω_{L}$, $ω_{M}$), and product demand gives $∂$ln Q/$∂$ln p = −$ε$. The cost shares are $s_{L}$ = $ω_{L}$·$C_{1}$/C and $s_{M}$ = $ω_{M}$·$C_{2}$/C, and the Allen–Uzawa elasticity of substitution is $σ$ = C·$C_{12}$/($C_{1}$·$C_{2}$). Because C is homogeneous of degree one, $C_{1}$ and $C_{2}$ are homogeneous of degree zero, so $ω_{L}$·$C_{11}$ + $ω_{M}$·$C_{12}$ = 0 and $ω_{L}$·$C_{12}$ + $ω_{M}$·$C_{22}$ = 0.

*The price of AI services.* Holding w and $a_{L}$ fixed,

$$
\frac{\partial \ln L}{\partial \ln \omega_M}
= -\varepsilon \, \frac{\omega_M C_2}{C} + \frac{\omega_M C_{12}}{C_1}
= s_M(\sigma - \varepsilon). \tag{A2}
$$

The second term uses $ω_{M}$·$C_{12}$/$C_{1}$ = $σ$·$ω_{M}$·$C_{2}$/C = $σ$·$s_{M}$.

*Labor-augmenting technical change.* Holding w and $ω_{M}$ fixed, $∂$ln $ω_{L}$/$∂$ln $a_{L}$ = −1, so $∂$ln p/$∂$ln $a_{L}$ = −$s_{L}$ and

$$
\frac{\partial \ln L}{\partial \ln a_L}
= \varepsilon \, s_L - \frac{\omega_L C_{11}}{C_1} - 1
= s_L(\varepsilon - 1) + s_M(\sigma - 1). \tag{A3}
$$

The second equality uses −$ω_{L}$·$C_{11}$/$C_{1}$ = $ω_{M}$·$C_{12}$/$C_{1}$ = $σ$·$s_{M}$ and $s_{L}$ + $s_{M}$ = 1. With labor as the only input ($s_{L}$ = 1), $L = Q/a_L$ and $L \propto a_L^{\varepsilon-1}$.

*Demand for AI services and the Jevons condition.* Physical AI services are M = Q·$C_{2}$/$a_{M}$. Using $ω_{M}$·$C_{22}$/$C_{2}$ = −$σ$·$s_{L}$, the elasticity of effective AI services with respect to their effective price is −($s_{L}$·$σ$ + $s_{M}$·$ε$), and

$$
\frac{\partial \ln M}{\partial \ln a_M} = s_L\,\sigma + s_M\,\varepsilon - 1. \tag{A4}
$$

Total use of AI services rises with their efficiency—Jevons's case—if and only if $s_{L}$·$σ$ + $s_{M}$·$ε$ \> 1. The same condition determines whether spending on AI services, $p_{M}$·M, rises when $p_{M}$ falls, because $∂$ln($p_{M}$·M)/$∂$ln $p_{M}$ = 1 − ($s_{L}$·$σ$ + $s_{M}$·$ε$). When $s_{M}$ is small, the condition is approximately $σ$ \> 1.

*Relation to Marshall's third law.* The own-price elasticity of labor demand is −[(1 − $s_{L}$)$σ$ + $s_{L}$·$ε$]. Its magnitude rises with labor's cost share if and only if $ε$ \> $σ$. This is Hicks's (1932) qualification of Marshall's (1890) rule that demand for an input is less elastic when the input accounts for a small share of cost. The same inequality determines the sign of (A2).

*Decomposition into scale and substitution effects.* Write ln L = ln Q + ln(L/Q). From pricing and product demand, $∂$ln Q/$∂$ln $ω_{M}$ = −$ε$·$s_{M}$, and from (A1), $∂$ln(L/Q)/$∂$ln $ω_{M}$ = $ω_{M}$·$C_{12}$/$C_{1}$ = $σ$·$s_{M}$. Defining $Δ$ln p as the change in log unit cost (to first order, $s_{M}$·$Δ$ln $ω_{M}$),

$$
\Delta \ln L = \underbrace{-\varepsilon\,\Delta \ln p}_{\Delta \ln Q} + \underbrace{\sigma\,\Delta \ln p}_{\Delta \ln (L/Q)},
\qquad \varepsilon = -\frac{\Delta \ln Q}{\Delta \ln p}, \quad \sigma = \frac{\Delta \ln (L/Q)}{\Delta \ln p}. \tag{A5}
$$

For the CES technology below, ln(L/Q) = constant + $σ$·ln C − $σ$·ln $ω_{L}$ − ln $a_{L}$, so with w and $a_{L}$ fixed, (A5) holds exactly for discrete changes when $Δ$ln p is the exact change in unit cost. If wages or labor productivity also change, $σ$ is the ratio of $Δ$ln($a_{L}$·L/Q) to $Δ$ln(C/$ω_{L}$). If pass-through to the output price is incomplete, $ε$ should be computed against the observed change in the output price and $σ$ against the change in unit cost.

*Factor shares.* For the CES technology $Q = [\alpha(a_L L)^{\rho} + (1-\alpha)(a_M M)^{\rho}]^{1/\rho}$, with $\sigma = 1/(1-\rho)$, cost minimization gives

$$
\frac{s_M}{s_L} = \left( \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \right)^{\sigma} \left( \frac{\omega_M}{\omega_L} \right)^{1-\sigma},
\qquad
\Delta \ln \frac{s_M}{s_L} = (1-\sigma)\,\Delta \ln \frac{\omega_M}{\omega_L}. \tag{A6}
$$

If $σ$ \> 1, a fall in $ω_{M}$ raises AI's share of cost and lowers labor's. The second expression is the estimating equation for the cost-share route in Table 2 of the article.

*Large price changes.* With CES technology, $s_L = \alpha^{\sigma}(\omega_L/C)^{1-\sigma}$, so with $\omega_L$ fixed, $\Delta\ln s_L = (\sigma-1)\,\Delta\ln C$. Substituting into (A5), with $Δ$ln p = $Δ$ln C, gives

$$
\Delta \ln L = \frac{\sigma - \varepsilon}{\sigma - 1} \, \Delta \ln s_L. \tag{A7}
$$

Because $s_{L}$ is close to one when AI's share is small, $Δ$ln $s_{L}$ is approximately the negative of the change in AI's share, so the employment effect is of the same order unless $σ$ is close to one or \|$σ$ − $ε$\| is large relative to \|$σ$ − 1\|. Figure 2 of the article plots $\exp\{[(\sigma-\varepsilon)/(\sigma-1)]\ln(1-s_M)\} - 1$, the change in employment as AI's share rises from zero to $s_{M}$. When $σ$ = 1, AI's share is constant and (A2) integrates directly to $Δ$ln L = $s_{M}$(1 − $ε$)·$Δ$ln $ω_{M}$, which can be large for a large price decline.

*Several types of labor.* Let the inputs be junior labor J, other labor, and AI services M, with cost shares $s_{k}$ and Allen partial elasticities of substitution $σ_{jk}$ = C·$C_{jk}$/($C_{j}$·$C_{k}$). The elasticity of the conditional demand for input j with respect to the effective price of input k is $s_{k}$·$σ_{jk}$, and homogeneity implies $s_{J}$·$σ_{JJ}$ = −$Σ_{k≠J}$ $s_{k}$·$σ_{Jk}$. Repeating the steps behind (A2) and (A3) gives

$$
\frac{\partial \ln L_J}{\partial \ln \omega_M} = s_M(\sigma_{JM} - \varepsilon), \tag{A8}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \ln L_J}{\partial \ln a_J} = s_J(\varepsilon - 1) + \sum_{k \neq J} s_k(\sigma_{Jk} - 1). \tag{A9}
$$

The expression in the article's section on junior workers writes the sum in (A9) as (1 − $s_{J}$)($σ_{J}$ − 1), where $σ_{J}$ is the cost-share-weighted average of the $σ_{Jk}$. Equation (A8) governs the agent channel and (A9) the copilot channel; the experiments discussed in the article estimate the productivity change that enters (A9), not $σ_{JM}$.

*A task-based interpretation.* Suppose that output combines a continuum of tasks with a cross-task elasticity of substitution $η$, and that each task is performed by labor or by AI, whichever is cheaper in that task. At a fixed assignment of tasks, the elasticity of substitution between labor and AI equals $η$. When AI's effective price falls, tasks at the margin switch from labor to AI, so the reduced-form $σ$ in (A2) exceeds $η$ by an amount that depends on how many tasks lie near the cost threshold (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2018). An advance in capabilities that makes new tasks feasible for AI at any price is not a movement along this schedule; in the reduced form it appears as a fall in labor's task content, $Γ$, in (A10) below.

*New tasks and the task content of production.* Following Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019), write the CES unit cost as $C^{1-\sigma} = \Gamma\,\omega_L^{1-\sigma} + (1-\Gamma)\,\omega_M^{1-\sigma}$, where $Γ$ is labor's task content of production. This is the technology behind (A6), with its distribution weights rescaled to sum to one. Shephard's lemma gives $C_1 = \Gamma\,\omega_L^{-\sigma} C^{\sigma}$, so by (A1), $\ln L = \ln Q + \ln\Gamma - \sigma\ln\omega_L + \sigma\ln C - \ln a_L$. With $w$ and $a_L$ fixed and $Q \propto C^{-\varepsilon}$,

$$
\Delta \ln L = (\sigma - \varepsilon)\,\Delta \ln C + \Delta \ln \Gamma, \tag{A10}
$$

which holds exactly for discrete changes. With $Γ$ fixed, (A10) reduces to (A5). A change in $Γ$ also changes unit cost: automating a task that AI performs more cheaply lowers both $Γ$ and C, so its effect on employment combines displacement ($Δ$ln $Γ$ \< 0) with a cost effect, ($σ$ − $ε$)·$Δ$ln C, which is positive when $ε$ \> $σ$. New tasks in which labor has the advantage raise $Γ$, the reinstatement effect. $Γ$ summarizes the structure of production; it is not an elasticity, and its path depends on the direction of innovation rather than on prices.

*Perfect substitution in a task.* If one unit of labor performs $a_{L}$ units of a task and one unit of AI services performs $a_{M}$ units, cost minimization assigns the task to labor only if w/$a_{L}$ $≤$ $p_{M}$/$a_{M}$, that is, w $≤$ ($a_{L}$/$a_{M}$)·$p_{M}$. Near this threshold, the cost saving from automating the task is small, so the price reduction that drives the scale effect in (A5) is small while the displacement of labor in the task is complete.

*Scope.* All results are for one competitive industry with constant returns and an exogenous wage. Apart from (A10), they abstract from new tasks; all of them abstract from reallocation across industries, and from changes in $σ$ itself, which the main text discusses.

## Online Appendix B: Coding of Table 1 and Two Case Studies

Table 1 of the article codes each episode on three dimensions, using the sources listed in the table.

*Scope automated.* An episode is coded as automating the entire function, or the core function, when the technology produced the output that defined the occupation without a member of the occupation operating it: traction for horses and mules, calculation for human computers, recording a likeness for miniature portrait painters, connecting calls for telephone operators, operating the car for elevator operators, and recording and reconciling transactions for bookkeeping clerks. It is coded as automating a subset of tasks when members of the occupation continued to perform other tasks that made up a substantial part of the job, as accountants did after the spreadsheet and tellers did after the automated teller machine. Online and mobile banking is grouped with whole-function substitution because it performs most routine transactions without a teller.

*Demand for output.* "Expanded" means that output of the good or service grew faster than the productivity gain the technology delivered, so that total input into the activity rose. "Grew less than productivity" means that output grew but total input fell, as in agriculture after mechanization. "No induced demand" means that the technology did not make the final service cheap enough to raise its consumption appreciably, as with elevator rides. The judgments are qualitative; no elasticities were estimated.

*Incumbent employment.* The change in employment of the incumbent occupation over the period shown, from the cited source.

*Placement in Figure 1.* Episodes coded as automating the entire or core function are placed in the row for whole-function substitution, and those coded as automating a subset of tasks in the row for partial automation. Episodes coded "Expanded" are placed in the column for demand growth, as is online and mobile banking, which the author codes as demand met through new channels rather than by tellers; farm mechanization and automatic elevators are placed in the other column. Tellers appear twice, under partial automation for 1988–2004 and under whole-function substitution after 2010, to illustrate the migration of an occupation between cells. Radiology and telephone switching are not placed, for the reasons given in the note to Figure 1.

The two case studies below expand on episodes in Table 1. Software development and photography both produced large expansions in demand, but they differ in which occupation performed the new work, which makes them a useful pair.

*Software development: the incumbent occupation gained.* Programming productivity has risen by large multiples. The first FORTRAN compiler, released in 1957, reduced the number of statements needed to program a machine by a factor of roughly 4 to 20; in one benchmark, 47 FORTRAN statements produced about 1,000 machine instructions (Backus et al. 1957). Compilers, open-source libraries, and cloud services have added further multiples since. Employment grew nevertheless. US employment in information technology occupations rose from 450,000 in 1970 to 4.6 million in 2014 (Beckhusen 2016), and software developers held about 1.7 million jobs in 2025 (BLS 2026c). Because employment equals output divided by output per worker, a tenfold rise in employment alongside large productivity gains implies that output grew by a far larger factor than output per worker.

Earnings rose with employment. Among men working full time, year-round, real median earnings in nonmanagerial information technology occupations rose from \$74,180 in 1970 to \$80,895 in 2014, in 2014 dollars, while median earnings for all such men fell from \$55,690 to \$49,150 (Beckhusen 2016). The content of the work has shifted toward design. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects that employment of computer programmers will fall about 7 percent between 2025 and 2035, while employment of software developers will rise about 10 percent (BLS 2026a, 2026c). Software development is the clearest historical case of complementary expansion, and it is now among the occupations most exposed to AI.

*Photography: a new occupation gained.* Photography did not raise the productivity of miniature portrait painters; it replaced them in recording a likeness. In the 1840s, a daguerreotype portrait cost \$2 to \$5 in most American towns (Nelson 1996), within reach of buyers who could not afford a painted miniature, and after the invention of the daguerreotype in 1839 many American miniaturists abandoned their art (Barratt 2004).

Demand for photographic portraits then grew rapidly. By one estimate, 300 to 400 million cartes de visite were sold each year in England in the early-to-mid 1860s (Darrah 1981, cited in Moran 2018), when cartes typically sold for \$2 to \$3 a dozen (Darrah 1981). Census returns record 51 professional photographers in Britain in 1851 (Shrimpton n.d.) and 2,366 people employed in photography in England and Wales in 1861 (Edwards 2006). The technology produced complementary expansion for image-making as an activity and whole-function substitution for the incumbent occupation. Unlike AI, however, it still required a human operator for every portrait.
